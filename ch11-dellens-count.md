# Chapter 11 — Dellen's Count

The clouds that stood behind the ridge on the day we buried Marit did nothing. They climbed all afternoon into a tall, bruised, magnificent anvil, and leaned out over the basin, and gave us six minutes of wind, a smell of hot stones, and a long dry rattle of thunder like furniture being moved in an upper room. Then they went east. The Almanac had given them eight percent. The gauge at the creamery caught nothing. Wren's gauge-readers, in the dawn, stood at their fence-posts and read the water, and wrote *0.0,* and wrote it again, and again, through the whole first week of August.

I note it because it is a thing the story does, and I have learned to be grateful to it. When the rain does not come, something has to be done with the days. We did what people do. We counted.

---

Dellen sent word through Hettie that he would like to see me at the feed store after hours, on the Tuesday, the eighth. He did not say why. Hettie, who had carried the message in her apron pocket for most of a day before she delivered it, gave it in her usual way, with her face turned slightly toward the window, and then said, "He's been up at night. I'd bring a cake."

I did not bring a cake. I brought a bottle of Odd's rye, which he gives me by the case, and which I have never been able to drink, and I walked down the road at six with it under my arm.

The Roque feed store stands on the corner of the main road and the grange lane, and it has stood there for four generations: a long low building with a false front, clad in tin that was painted green in Dellen's grandfather's time and has since faded to the color of a pond. Inside it smells, as it always has, of molasses and bone meal and leather and dust. There are sacks stacked to the rafters. There is a counter of scarred pine with a brass scale on it and a cash drawer that has not shut properly since I was a girl, and over it, on a nail, the framed certificate from the college in the capital, and the photograph of his father, and the calendar from the seed company, still turned to May.

He had put the CLOSED sign in the window. He was sitting on a stool behind the counter with a ledger open in front of him and a pencil behind his ear, and when I came in he looked up, and I saw that he had not slept.

"You brought rye," he said.

"I brought rye."

"I don't drink it." But he took it and set it on the shelf behind him, beside a tin of liniment, with a care that told me it would not be opened, and would not be thrown away. "I'd like to show you something. I've not shown it to anyone."

He turned the ledger around.

It was a list. It was a long, ruled, hand-drawn list, in the neat square hand of a man who has kept books for twenty-five years, of the two hundred and twelve households of the Carrow Water Cooperative, in the order of the roll. Beside each name were three columns. The first was headed *S.* The second, *N.* The third, with a small unsteady question mark, was left without a heading at all. And in each row but one, in pencil, in a very small hand, there was a single tick.

I read down the first page. *Aldecott, T.* There was no tick in any column. *Bremmer, O.* A tick in *N,* underlined. *Fenn, A.* A tick in *S.* *Harlan, J.* A tick in *S.* *Lindqvist, G.* A tick in the third column, with a note beside it that said *teacher.*

"What is it?" I said.

"It's the vote." He took the pencil from behind his ear. "It's what I think the vote is, as of tonight. I've done it by the roll. I know every one of them. I know their balance at the store and the size of their loan and what their wife said when they came in for twine. I've been keeping it since May." He tapped the bottom of the last page. "A hundred and eighteen yes. Sixty-one no. Thirty-three I can't call."

I looked at the numbers. I did the arithmetic, as one does. "You need a hundred and forty-two."

"For the sale. Two-thirds of all, absence counts as no." He nodded. "So the yeses need another twenty-four of the thirty-three. Which is — I've gone round and round this. It's a lot. It's three in four of the ones who haven't decided. But I'll tell you what. If I were Meridian, I'd be sleeping fine."

"Why?"

"Because of the heat." He said it simply. "A hundred and eighteen in May was ninety. We'd had a good winter for the idea of staying. We'd had a lot of *better than one in five.* And then we had July, and we had Marit's well, and the Fenns' cistern, and nine of your hives in a trench. The ones in the middle can feel it. They don't need a number. They can see that the ground's the color of a plate." He put the pencil down. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you."

"Then why?"

"I'm telling you because I want you to read it straight on a Thursday." He spoke carefully. "Not *better than one in five.* Twelve. And the interval. And the date it hits dead pool, and what that does to the tier. All of it. In your own voice, in the grange, so that every one of the thirty-three hears the same thing at the same hour and can't say afterward that nobody told them."

"That's what Odd asked."

"It is." Dellen looked at me. "And I'd like you to know that I'm not sure it's what I'd want. If I were you."

I sat down on the second stool. It was a low thing, with a split seat, and it had been put there, I imagine, for the use of old men who came in to talk about prices.

"Say that again."

"I don't know which way it cuts," said Dellen. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. I've done the sums every way I know. If you say twelve, some of my thirty-three say *it's over,* and sell. That's the Bell Year. That's fourteen, and the bank, and the bell. I know that. I was a young man behind that counter and I remember my father's face. And some of the yeses I've got, the hundred and eighteen, some of them say yes only because they think it's one in five and they can't stand the wait. If they hear twelve, and the interval, and the *four to twenty-four*, they might think: *four. It could be four. It could be twenty-four. It could be rain.* And some of those might turn."

"Which?"

"I don't know." He spread his hands. "I've asked myself a hundred times. Does a true number make people braver or does it make them run? I'd have said run. I was sure of it. In the Bell Year I'd have said it for the rest of my life. But I've been reading the footers for two months, Tamsin. Hettie let me have the file, every sheet she's kept since the Resolution, sixteen years of Fridays, and I went through the small print on every one. And I'll tell you what I found."

"What?"

"The footer's been there for sixteen years," said Dellen, "and nobody's run."

I did not speak.

"It's an experiment. That's what I keep thinking. For sixteen years there's been a real number on the wall in seven-point type and not one soul in the basin has been driven off by it, because not one soul has read it." He gave a short unamused laugh. "So it tells me nothing. It's a control with no subject. That's what Joss would say. I don't know whether the number's dangerous. I only know that we've never once let it be *heard.* And I think if you're going to decide a thing like this, for two hundred and twelve households, on the strength of a *guess* about what the number does, you ought to find out."

"By saying it."

"By saying it," said Dellen. "Once. Straight. And then we'll know."

He had put the ledger down. He sat with his hands flat on the counter on either side of it, a large, tired, balding man in a feed-store apron, and I saw in his face the thing that I had seen on the strip in May and had not understood. It was not anger. It was not even grief. It was a deep and exhausted fairness, like a scale that has been left too long on a shelf, and will not be moved now by either hand.

"Where do you stand?" I said. "On the sale?"

"I don't know."

"You're a yes."

"I'm a *question mark,*" said Dellen. "It's in the book." He turned the ledger on its spine, and there, on the last row, under *Roque, D.,* in the third column, in the very small pencil, was a single tick.

I looked at him.

"Sixteen years I've known the number," said Dellen. "I've read the footers. I've given a man a feed store's worth of credit because I thought it'd hold. I thought it'd *hold.* And I've never once been told the truth about anything by the one person in the basin who has it." He picked up the pencil and put it back behind his ear. "That's not a complaint. It's a fact about the weather. But I'd like, once, to make up my own mind."

---

It was Joss who told me about Ilka.

He came to the creamery on the Wednesday morning at six, before I had arrived, and I found him sitting in the chair with the lamp on and the terminal asleep, and his hands folded on the shelf in front of him like a man in a waiting room. He had not shaved. There was a sheet of paper under his hands, and when I came in he stood up, like someone who had been up all night preparing an apology, and said, "Mrs. Aldecott, I would like to tell you something, and then I'd like to resign."

"Don't be foolish."

"I'm not. It's —" He put the paper into my hand. "I've been through the technical appendix. To the Meridian offer. It's what I was supposed to be doing, in the first place. Reviewing the hydrology. I'd put it off. It's four hundred pages. It's the section that gives Meridian's own forecast, the thing the whole price is built on, and I'd put it off because I thought it'd be a copy of the Almanac's numbers with a different font. And it isn't. It's good. It's — it's very good. It's a water balance from the 'sixties, with three scenarios and a sensitivity analysis. It's *careful.*" His voice caught. "And on the last page, in the credits, there's a name."

I looked at the paper. It was a photocopy of a single page, the last of the appendix, with a short list of authors and a date. The date was the second of May. And beneath it, in the plain gray type that the college used for everything it did not wish to be read, there was a line:

*Technical review: I. Aldecott-Prasad, consultant (independent), for Meridian Basin Holdings.*

I stood in the room with the page in my hand. It was very bright in the creamery. The first sun was just coming through the south window and had laid a wide gold bar across the wall of rain cards, so that each card flared, one after the other, like a row of lit windows at the end of a street, and I thought, with an absurd small clarity, that it was beautiful.

"The second of May," I said.

"She came on the twenty-fifth of April."

"Yes."

"She told you she'd declined."

"She told me she'd said she would decline. And then thought about it." I heard my own voice, flat and dry. "I remember the sentence. It was a very *careful* sentence."

"I'm so sorry," said Joss.

"Why did you tell me?"

"Because I'm the one who found it." He was crying again, a little, quietly and humiliatedly, a boy who had never learned to do it with dignity. "Because you'd be told by Meridian's people in October, in the hall, with everyone there. Because I'd put it in the report and you'd read it. Because I thought, if it was me, I'd want to hear it from someone who — who didn't enjoy it."

I put the page on the shelf. I smoothed it, once, with the flat of my hand.

"You'll not resign," I said. "I need an auditor."

---

I found her in the orchard at nine, on her knees in the long grass under the old pear tree, with the field kit open on a tarp and the little folding frame of the weather station half assembled in front of her. She had her sleeves pushed up. She was threading a wire through a bracket, with the small, concentrated, impatient frown that she has worn over a task since she was four, and she did not hear me until I was almost at her shoulder.

"Mama."

"Ilka."

"I'm putting up a station. I've been meaning to. There's a gap between the creamery and Odd's, the Almanac says, a kind of blind spot in the —" She stopped. She looked up at my face. She put the wire down, very slowly, on the tarp, and sat back on her heels, and for a moment neither of us moved.

"He found it," she said.

"He found it."

"I thought he would. I thought it would be him." She wiped her hands on her trousers. "I've been meaning to tell you. I tried. Three times. I'd get as far as the *C* in *consultant.* You were so —" She did not finish.

"You told me you'd declined."

"I told you I'd *said* I would. I said I'd think about it. I did think about it." She was looking at me squarely. There was no defiance in her face, and no shame. There was a flat, tired, appalling honesty, the honesty of having stopped bargaining. "I took the contract on the twenty-fourth of April, the day before I came home. I'd had the offer for a month. I wrote the hydrology in a week, at your table, and sent it in on the second of May. I told the Authority the day after. They put me on leave."

"On leave."

"They put me on *leave,* Mama. They didn't give me a choice. A senior hydrologist at the Coastal Water Authority takes a paid consultancy from a company that's buying the water rights of a basin she's a — that she's from — it's a conflict. It's the clearest conflict I've ever had. I knew it was. I did it anyway." She took a breath. "I haven't got a job. Not really. I've got a notice."

"You came home."

"I came home because I had nowhere else I wanted to be." Her voice shook, once, and steadied. "And because I'd written a document that I thought was true, and I wanted to see what it did."

"You said you'd read the numbers honestly."

"I did read them honestly. It's the most honest thing I've ever written." She turned her hands up on her knees. "That's what I need you to understand. I didn't do it for money. I didn't do it to hurt you. I did it because I looked at the Almanac's numbers, and I looked at the climate scenarios, and I did the sums, and the answer is that in fifteen years this basin will not have enough water to farm. I mean *will not.* It isn't a question of a bad year. It's a structural deficit. In another twenty it's worse. And what I thought was: the best thing that can happen to these people is that somebody pays them a fair price for water they're going to lose anyway, while they've still got something to sell."

"So you worked for Meridian."

"I worked for the *numbers.* The numbers are what they are."

"You rounded it," I said.

I do not know why I said it. It came out of me with the sudden low thrust of a thing that has been waiting. It was cruel, and it was precise, and she flinched as if I had touched a burn.

"I rounded it," said Ilka, very quietly. "Yes. I did. I said *declined.* I let you think I'd declined, because I knew what you'd say. I did exactly what you taught me." She lifted her chin. "Is that what you want me to say? That you made me? That I learned it at your table?"

"I'm not —"

"Because I did. I did learn it at your table. I learned that you tell people the true thing in a shape they can hold. And I held you in a shape you could hold. I thought, *she can't take this today.* I thought, *I'll tell her after she's had the bees.*" She laughed, short and harsh. "And I did tell you, Mama. In the end. I told you I was a conflict of interest, in the kitchen, the first night. I said it in a way you could hear. And you heard it. You just heard it as *a colleague.*"

I stood in the long grass. The pear tree dropped a single small hard green fruit, with a soft thud, between us.

"The sale is the right thing," said my daughter. "I believe that. I'd say it to the whole hall. Whatever you read on a Thursday, however you read it. The numbers say the basin is going to run dry, and the choice isn't *sell or stay.* The choice is *sell now, with two hundred and seventy thousand dollars and a house, or sell in ten years, to the bank, with nothing.* That's Odd's whole life. That's Cormac Teague's baby. And I know — I know — you think I'm the enemy. I know you think it's about the water. It isn't. It's about *them.*"

"It's about the thing that makes the basin what it is."

"It's about whether anyone's alive in it in twenty years." She was on her feet now. I had not seen her rise. "Papa wrote you a letter saying, *do it as yourself.* I'm doing it as myself. I'm saying what I think. Out loud. Where you can hear."

"And I'm supposed to —"

"I don't know what you're supposed to do." She wiped her face with her wrist. "I only know that for fourteen years you've been the most honest liar in the basin. And I'd rather be a dishonest truth-teller. At least it's *mine.*"

It was a good line. I have thought about it a great deal. It was a line that Ruben might have written. It had the cut of his hand in it, the plain unsparing exactness, and as it fell into the grass between us I felt the whole of my love for her turn over, once, in my chest, like a stone at the bottom of a pool.

"Go and pack," I said.

I did not mean it as it sounded. I think she knew that. But she heard it, and her face went quite white, and she gathered up the field kit in four efficient movements, folded the tarp, and went away through the orchard toward the house without looking back.

---

They left on the Friday, on the morning bus. School began on the twenty-first and there was nothing left for Wren here, said Ilka, in a clear flat voice, over breakfast, but a week of being asked questions he could not answer. I did not argue. I had said enough. I stood at the head of the lane in the pale dawn with a paper bag of Odd's rye bread and a jar of honey, and watched my daughter hand up the duffel and the long case, as if she had done this before and promised herself it would be different.

She did not kiss me. She held me, briefly, and let go. "I'll be back for the vote," she said.

"You don't need to."

"I'm a household," said Ilka. "I've got a share. It's my right." She smiled, a thin tired smile, and for a second I saw her at nineteen, on this same road, with a suitcase. "I won't be on the side you think. But I'll be here."

Wren had been very quiet. He stood with his helmet in one hand and the notebook under his arm, looking up at me with his long dark far-apart eyes, and he did the thing that I did not expect. He put the notebook into my hands.

"It's for you," he said. "It's not finished."

"What is it?"

"It's the counts." He rubbed his nose with the back of his wrist. "From the hives. And the gauges. I did a page for each of the days. I put the numbers in, and I put the words. Like Mr. Bremmer." He hesitated. "There's one page I left blank. It says *Reader's note.* You can fill it in. If you want."

"What do I put in it?"

"What it's for," said Wren. He climbed up the bus step. At the top he turned and looked down at me, small and exact in his green sweater, in the long cool light of the morning, and he said: "Mama says you say it differently. But I think you're the only one who knows how it's supposed to sound."

The door folded shut. Pell, at the wheel, gave me a slow nod. The bus went away up the road toward the ridge, with the dust lifting behind it in a long pale plume, and I stood at the head of my lane with a notebook against my chest until the sound of it had gone out of the air.

I did not open it that day. I carried it to the creamery, and put it on the shelf beside the printer, between the green book and the yellow paper, and sat in the chair with my hands in my lap for a long time. Out of the corner of my eye the cabinet pulsed its small green light. On the wall the hundred and twelve rain cards glowed in the morning, each with its tack, each with its number, and one of them, I saw, in the third row, from the year of the Bell, had a word beside the number that I had never noticed before, written in the margin in very small pencil.

It said, *the bell.* It was the thirty-first of March.
