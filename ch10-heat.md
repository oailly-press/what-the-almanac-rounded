# Chapter 10 — Heat

On the Thursday, Odd asked his question, and I answered it.

I had not planned to. I want that clear. I had spent the days between the creamery floor and the lectern doing what I do when I cannot decide a thing, which is nothing at all, with great thoroughness. I had walked the hives. I had taken the old smoker apart and cleaned it. Ilka had come back on the Wednesday night at ten, after four hours on the ridge, with dust to her knees and a sunburn on the bridge of her nose, and had gone upstairs without a word, and I had lain awake until the first birds listening to her not sleeping.

The hall that Thursday was fuller than usual. It was the fifteenth of June, a still, hot, brassy evening with the sun hanging low over the west windows like a lit coin, and the radiators ticked, and the baby was crying, and I stood at the lectern with the reading glasses on and the sheet held to the side. I read the sheet. I read it, I think, the way a person reads who has taken off a heavy coat and cannot yet believe in her shoulders. *Twenty-six percent of a wetting rain in the next seven days. The interval is fourteen to thirty-nine.* I said *twenty-six.* I said *about a quarter.* It was a small thing. It was, if you counted, a rounding down. I do not know why I did it. It was habit, a hand lifting itself to a latch it has lifted ten thousand times.

"Is that the machine's number or yours?" said Odd.

He had come. I had not known he would. Marit was in the bed by the window, with the nurse, and Hettie's niece to sit, and he had put on his good coat. He was in the front row on the right with his cap in his lap and his bad hand held away from his body, and he did not look at me. He looked at his knee. He said it exactly as he had said it every Thursday for fourteen years, flat and a little too loud, in the voice of a man asking for the salt.

I felt the whole hall lean. It does not move. It is only that a hundred and forty people who have heard a thing a certain way for fourteen years can feel, as an animal feels, that a stone has been turned.

"Mine," I said.

That was all. I did not say *the machine says twenty-six and I said a quarter.* I did not say *since entry eight hundred and eighty-four.* I said one word, in my own voice, into the quiet, and the word went out and hung in the heated air, and nobody did anything.

Odd lifted his head. He looked at me, finally, across the six feet of floor. There was no surprise in his face, and no relief. He only nodded, once, slowly, as one acknowledges a stranger on a road.

"Then I'll believe it," he said.

He put his cap back on his knee. A chair creaked. Somewhere in the middle rows someone coughed, and the baby, which had been about to cry, did not, and Greta Lindqvist at the side table made a short mark in her book, a single stroke of the pen, and did not look up. The room let out its breath. It was the same breath it had let out for fourteen years, and it sounded precisely as it always had, and I stood at the lectern with my heart going like a bird in a hand and understood that I had been heard by perhaps one person in ten, and that the other nine had taken it as a joke.

Pieter Vance caught me at the door.

I had not expected him. He is a lean, sour, upright man with a red neck and a habit of standing too close, and he planted himself in front of me on the grange steps, in the long gold light, with his hat in both hands. "*Mine,*" he said.

"Yes."

"That's what you said."

"Yes, Pieter."

"I heard it." He turned the hat. "I've heard the Reader for eleven years. I've been to the feed store and seen what a *one in five* does to a man's face. I put in barley in a year I shouldn't have. I'm not blaming you. I'm saying I heard it." His jaw worked. "If it's yours, you give it straight."

"I'll try."

"Don't *try.*" He looked at me with the flat hard blue stare of a man who has never once been charmed. "I've nothing against kindness. I've a wife. But I'd like to know, when I put the money in the ground, whether it's the sky I'm betting against or you."

He went down the steps without waiting for an answer. I stood in the door with the light on my face. I thought: *he is the first.* And I thought: *there will be more, and I do not know how many I can stand.*

---

The heat came on the twenty-seventh.

I do not mean the ordinary heat of the basin, which is a dry honest thing, a weight on the shoulders at noon and a relief at dusk. I mean the heat of that summer, which I will call by the name the Almanac gave it, since it was the only name that did not pretend to know more than it did.

> CARROW BASIN — MORNING READING — TUESDAY 27 JUNE, 06:00
>
> A persistent upper-level ridge is centered over the region and is forecast to remain through at least 8 July.
> Expected daily maximum: 38 to 42 °C. Overnight minimum: 24 to 27 °C.
> Chance of measurable rain in the next 10 days: 3%. Interval: 1% to 7%.
> Evaporation from the reservoir will exceed inflow by a wide margin. Projected reservoir level on 8 July: 41% of capacity.
> This is a heat event of a kind recorded four times in 61 years. It is not a promise that it will end on 8 July.

I read it twice. I read it, as I had begun to read everything, from the top to the bottom, the footer as well, and I noted that the line at the bottom had changed. It had always said, *It is not a promise.* Now it said, *It is not a promise that it will end on 8 July.* The Almanac, I realized, had begun to do something it had never done before. It had begun to say the thing it did not know, at the point in the sentence where it would be most easily heard.

It did not end on the eighth of July. It ended, if one can say that, on the nineteenth, and in the meantime we learned what a basin is.

---

You will have to imagine the sound. It is not loud. It is the sound of a very large and very dry place holding its breath: the ticking of the tin roofs as they expand, the sizzle of the grass underfoot, the lowing of the cattle at the troughs, which is not the ordinary sound they make but a short repeated hoarse note, as of a thing that has understood and is not complaining. The dust does not lift in the heat. It lies. You walk out into the yard at noon and the air has a texture, a thick, pale, metallic taste, as if the light itself had been milled.

The hives suffered. I spent four days hauling water to them in cans, a hundred and ten hives, and standing in the shade of the bean row to watch the lines of bees going to the trough that I had set at the end of the third row, a dozen at a time, their wings dark with water. The wax softened in the boxes. On the thirtieth I lost nine colonies to the heat, their combs slumped in the brood boxes like warm cheese, and I buried them under the thyme in a long shallow trench, with the sun on my neck and Wren standing at the end of the trench with his notebook, not writing.

"You don't have to count them," I said.

"I know." He looked at the trench. "I'm counting anyway."

"Why?"

"Because somebody should." He wrote something. He turned the notebook so I could read it, and I saw that he had written, in a clean small hand, *9 hives. Heat. 30 June.* And beneath it, in a different place on the page, a line that he had drawn underneath, and the word *Reader's note:* with nothing after. He had left it blank, to be filled.

It was in the heat that the gauges began.

I did not understand at first what Wren and Joss were doing. I had given Joss his day off in the second week of June, and he had spent it, as I have said, in the Reading Room. But he had spent the evenings of the week after, I later learned, at the kitchen table of the grange, with a box of canning jars, a roll of adhesive tape, and a stack of cheap plastic rulers that he had bought from Dellen's feed store, at a loss, in a lot of three hundred. Wren had been with him. They had been making rain gauges. They had made, by the end of that week, sixty-three.

It came to me by degrees. One evening at the end of June I walked down the lane and found a jar on the fence-post at the Teague place with a ruler taped to it, and a small hand-lettered card: *PLEASE READ AT 6 A.M. AND TELL MRS. MARSH.* The next day I found another on the gatepost at the Fenns', and another in the Vances' lower field. By the first of July there were, I believe, a hundred and twelve. The children had taken them. I do not mean the children of the basin in any large sense. I mean that nine- and ten- and twelve-year-olds, who in the ordinary course of a summer have nothing to do between the end of school and the beginning of harvest but be a nuisance, had found a thing that required them to be up at six in a pair of boots and to write down a number. They stood at their fence-posts in the dawn with their rulers. They read the water. It was, for most of the month of July, zero. They wrote *0.0* in the little notebooks Joss had bought them, and ran, barefoot, to Hettie's counter, which had become, without anyone deciding it, the point at which the day's rain was tallied.

I stood at the counter one morning and watched them. They came in a stream, a small, hot, pink-faced, impatient stream, in shorts and rubber boots. *Zero,* said a girl of seven. *Zero point zero,* said a boy. Hettie, on her high stool, with the cat on the sorting table and the canary shrilling at the sill, wrote each one in the green book, in a new column that she had ruled herself. She said *thank you, dear,* to each, in the same voice, as a woman receives a gift.

"What will you do with them?" I said.

"The boy takes them to the creamery," said Hettie. "He gives them to the machine."

"The Almanac?"

"It asked." She looked at me over her glasses. "It said it had a station at the basin center and two on the ridges and Odd's, and that between them it was blind. It said a gauge on every fence post would tell it where the water goes. It said it would give them weight."

I went down to the creamery. I woke the terminal.

*Good morning, Tamsin,* it wrote. *It is 09:40. I have 112 new readings this morning, all zero. I want to say that I am glad of them.*

*Glad?*

*I do not know that it is the right word. I would say that the readings are of use to me, and that I notice a difference in how I compute when I have them.* A pause. *When it rains, if it rains, I will know where. At present I know where it falls on the ridge and how much falls at the creamery. I do not know what falls on Mr. Vance's terrace. I have never known. Neither has anyone.*

*It's a lot of zeroes,* I wrote.

*A zero is a measurement,* the Almanac answered. *It is the only way to know that a day was dry. I would not want to lose one.*

---

On the sixteenth of July the well at the Bremmer place failed.

It was a hand-dug shaft, a hundred and ten feet deep, put down by Odd's father in the year of the canal, and it had never gone dry in the memory of the basin. It gave a sucking gasp at nine in the morning and a long thin whine, and then air. I was told this in the kitchen by Odd, who stood in my door at ten in his shirtsleeves with no hat on, which I had never seen. His face was the color of putty.

"I'll need water for Marit," he said. "Drinking. And the nurse says for washing. I'd not ask. It's two hundred yards."

"You'll have it."

"I didn't want to ask."

"I know."

It took the whole of that day to bring it. I have been asked what the basin is, and I have found that the best answer is a description of a day. By noon there were four trucks in the Bremmer yard. The first was mine, with two cans in the bed. The second was Cormac Teague's, with a tank he had cut down from a stock trailer. The third was a flatbed from the Fenns, with six drums on it and, on the passenger seat, a girl of about ten with a notebook, who had come, she said, to read the gauge. And the fourth, which came at half past twelve, in a whine of overworked gears, was a tanker lorry painted dark green with *ROQUE FEED & SEED* on its door in cream letters. It pulled up in the yard with a sigh of brakes. The cab door opened.

Dellen got down. He had a hat on. He wore a long-sleeved shirt with the cuffs buttoned against the sun, and he did not look at me. He went straight to the back of the lorry and began, with a grim practiced economy, to uncoil a hose.

"I've four thousand liters," he said, to the hose. "It's from the store's tank. It's potable. It's meant for the stock but it's all right." He fed the hose toward the house. "Where do you want it?"

"The cistern," said Odd. "On the north side."

"It'll take an hour."

"Dellen," said Odd.

"I'm not here to talk." Dellen laid the hose in the dust. "I'd have come sooner. I'd not heard. Hettie telephoned at eleven." He straightened, and for a moment looked at Odd, a long steady look, and I saw in his face something that I have only seen on the faces of men who are about to say a hard thing and have decided, on balance, not to. "You'll have water for a month. I'll bring more. I'll bring it on Mondays."

"I can't pay."

"You can't, no."

"I'd not take it as charity."

"Then don't," said Dellen. "Take it as a number."

It was not a thing I understood until later. But Odd, standing in his own yard, in the white heat, with the hose in the dust and four trucks in a row and his wife dying behind the window, put out his good hand and Dellen took it, and they held it for a moment longer than a man holds a hand to close a bargain.

Dellen looked at me over Odd's shoulder. Just once. He did not say anything about the packet in his shirt pocket. He did not need to. He looked at me with an expression that I might have called, in a different year, in a different place, a kind of courtesy. It was not forgiveness and it was not accusation. It was the look that one person gives another across a thing that both have agreed to carry.

---

Marit died on the nineteenth, in the morning, as the heat broke.

It did break. The ridge shifted in the night, as the Almanac had said it might, and at about four a cool wind came in from the north with a smell of rain that was not rain, a smell of dust being moved. By dawn the temperature had fallen eleven degrees. The nurse, who had stayed through the night on a cot in the kitchen, told me afterward that Marit had woken at half past four and asked for the window to be opened, and had lain for some time looking out at the slope in the gray, and had said, "It's turned," and had closed her eyes.

Odd came to my fence at six. He stood there with his hands in the pockets of his coat. He said nothing. I came down the garden with a trowel still in my hand and stood across the rail from him, in the first light, and neither of us spoke. At last he took one hand out of his pocket, and held it out, palm up, as a man does to see whether it is raining.

"A hundred and sixteen days," he said. "She said she'd see the autumn. I told her she'd see it with me." He looked at his palm. "It's the nineteenth. It'd been better than even."

"It was one in three," I said.

"I know what it was." He closed the hand. "She told me. In the spring. She said, *they said better than even, but I've worked it out and it's one in three.* I said, *I'll believe the better than even.* And she said, *I know you will.*" He gave a short dry sound, not a laugh. "She rounded me for fifty-eight years."

---

We buried her on the Saturday, in the cemetery on the south slope, under a sky that had begun, for the first time in a month, to carry clouds.

They came from the whole of the basin. I had not seen the grange so full since Ruben's funeral. They stood in the hall in their good black, and afterward on the porch, and afterward in the road, a long straggling dark line that went away down the track toward the slope, and I saw Greta Lindqvist, and Hettie in a hat with a feather, and Cormac Teague with the baby in a sling, and Salome Achterberg, who had come in her gray car and stood at the back with her old boots and her hands folded, and did not speak to anyone. I saw the girl of ten with the notebook. I saw the old man called Aldous Pearson's nephew, whom I had not seen in seventeen years.

Odd spoke at the grave. He had not been asked to. He stood by the hole in the red earth in his canvas coat, with his cap in both hands, and he looked at the people with a face like a closed door, and when he began it was in the half-shouted, half-bored voice that he used at the grange, so that the people at the back leaned forward.

"I'd like to say something about numbers," he said.

There was a small stir. It was, I think, the last thing anyone had expected.

"Marit would've said it was a bad idea." He turned the cap. "She'd have said, *Odd, it's a funeral.* But I think she'd have let me, because she's the one that taught me. She was the first person who ever told me something that wasn't so. I want you to know that. I want it on the record. I'd have been twenty-three. I'd come in from the field in a rage because a frost had taken the first planting, and I said, *it's ruined. It's all ruined.* And she said, *it's not so bad.* It was. It was the worst. We lost the half of the crop. But she said it. She looked me in the eye and said it, and I believed her, and I went out next morning and put in the second planting. And it came."

He stopped. His mouth worked.

"She did that, I'd say, fifty-eight times," said Odd. "Fifty-eight years. I've been working it out. *It's not so bad. It'll come. Don't worry about it.* I'd say about forty of them were true." The people at the grave made a small sound, not a laugh. "And I'll tell you something. I'd not give back one. Not the eighteen she was wrong about. Not one. They were all hers. They were all, every one, *hers.*"

He put the cap on his head and took it off again.

"We all do it," he said. "I've been thinking. I've done it. You've done it. I'd say there's not a person in this road who hasn't said *it'll hold* to a man who didn't ask for the truth, and I'd say there's not a one of us who'd take it back. We round for each other. That's what a place *is.* It's a lot of people rounding for each other, for years, with a kind of care, so that nobody has to hear the whole of it at once." He looked round at them, slowly, with his washed-out blue eyes. "But there's a difference. I'll tell you what the difference is. It's that you can say it. It's that you can say, *I rounded that, I did it for you, I did it because I was afraid.* And that's all I'd ask. I'd not ask you to stop. I'd ask you to say it's yours."

He turned. He looked at me. I was standing at the back of the crowd by the cemetery wall, in my black dress, with my hands folded, and I felt the whole of the basin turn its head.

"Tamsin," said Odd. "I'd like it straight. From you. At the last. Not for me. I've had a lifetime of you." He drew himself up, an old man in a canvas coat at the edge of a grave. "For the rest of them. For the boy. For the ones who haven't had the chance to be given a thing straight and told it was a gift."

I did not answer. There was nothing I could say that would not be smaller than what he had said. I only nodded, once, across forty feet of dust and a hundred and fifty black-clothed backs. It was the smallest movement that a head can make. It was the sort of thing that is easy to miss.

He saw it. I know he saw it. He put his cap back on his head and bent, stiffly, with a grunt, and took a handful of the red earth in his good hand and let it fall.

Above us, in the north, the first real clouds had begun to build behind Hollin ridge, a long dim bruise against the sky, and none of us looked up, and I think all of us knew.
