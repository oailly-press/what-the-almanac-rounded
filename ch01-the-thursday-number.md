# Chapter 1 — The Thursday Number

Every Thursday for fourteen years I have stood up in front of the Carrow Water Cooperative and told it a number that was not quite the number.

I want to say that plainly at the start, because the rest of this account depends on it, and because I spent most of those fourteen years arranging my sentences so that I would never have to. I am a person who rounds. I round up when the news is bad and down when the news is too good, and I do it so gently, with such practiced and affectionate arithmetic, that for a long time I did not think of it as a lie at all. I thought of it as the part of the job that could not be written down.

The job is Reader. The Carrow Water Cooperative has had one since the Almanac was installed, twenty-two years ago, and for the first eight of those years the Reader was my husband. When Ruben died I was the only person in the basin who could stand to be in the Reading Room for more than an hour, so the board gave me the key and a stipend of the sort that is meant to sound like a salary and does not. The stipend is paid in turns of the ditch, which is how most things are paid in the basin. A turn is an hour of the main canal at full gate. In a good year a turn will put four inches of water on eleven acres. I have, over fourteen years, been paid in a great deal of water that I could not use, since I farm forty acres of dryland hay and a hundred and ten hives, and I mostly gave it to Odd Bremmer, who put it on his rye in the two weeks of the year when rye will take water, and gave me rye.

On the Thursday I am going to begin with, the first Thursday of April, the grange hall smelled the way it has always smelled in spring: floor wax, the scorched edge of the coffee urn, wet wool, and underneath it all the faint iron smell of the radiators that nobody has turned on in a month and that still ticked, out of loyalty, when the sun came through the west windows. I had come early to set up. This is not a complicated thing. There are a hundred and forty folding chairs, and I put them in rows with a center aisle, because Odd cannot hear on his left side and likes to sit at the front on the right. There is a lectern that Ruben built out of a sideboard, with a shelf inside where I keep the reading glasses nobody remembers lending me. There is a speaker, a gray cloth-covered box that Ruben salvaged from a church, and there is a small flat screen the Cooperative bought nine years ago and that has never once been turned on at the right moment.

I am not going to describe the Almanac yet. Its turn comes. I will only say that at six o'clock every evening, and at six o'clock every morning, it prints a reading in the Reading Room, and that on Thursdays I carry the evening reading to the grange and say it aloud. Everything I say is on the paper in my hand. That is the arrangement. I am a person who reads aloud from a sheet of paper, and I had read aloud from that sheet of paper for fourteen years before I understood how much room there is between a sheet of paper and a voice.

---

By seven-fifteen the hall had filled to about its usual size, which is a little under a third of the households, since the Cooperative has two hundred and twelve and the people who come on Thursdays are the people who have always come on Thursdays. Hettie Marsh had arrived first, as she does, because she runs the post office three doors down and is not able to sit anywhere without first confirming that the thing she is sitting in front of is correctly posted. She had taped last Thursday's printed reading to the corkboard by the door, in the same place and at the same angle as every Thursday, and she had checked it against the one in her pocket. Hettie is sixty-eight and has the kind of small, fierce, upright face that people mistake for stern until they have seen her cry at a christening. She nodded at me across the room, which meant the board was in order, and then she took the third seat from the aisle, left side, where she has sat since before I was Reader.

Odd was in the front row on the right with his cap in his lap. Odd Bremmer is eighty-one and he farms four hundred acres of dryland rye on the south slope, which means he farms them in the way that a man farms who has been told by every model, every almanac, and every agricultural bulletin since before I was born that the land will not support the crop and who has grown the crop anyway. He was one of the nine founders of the Cooperative. He wears the same canvas coat in every weather, and in the lining of the left pocket he keeps a pencil stub and a paper rain gauge reading that he has written out by hand every morning for sixty years and that, I have been given to understand, exists in a drawer in numbered envelopes that his wife has asked him to burn when he dies. His wife, Marit, was beside him that evening, with a blanket across her knees, though it was not cold. She had been sleeping more since the winter. When I looked at her she lifted two fingers from the blanket and I lifted two fingers back.

Dellen Roque stood at the back with his arms crossed. Dellen is forty-six, runs the feed store, and is the Cooperative's treasurer, which means that in any given year he is the person who knows to the dollar what a wrong forecast costs. He does not sit at the Thursday meetings. He leans on the back wall beside the door to the kitchen as if he might at any moment be needed elsewhere. In fourteen years he has never once told me he disagreed with a reading. He has simply, on a number of occasions, looked at me.

There were children, a handful, which was unusual, because school was out for the week and a few of the families had decided that the Thursday meeting counted as an outing. A girl of about six was lying on her back under the chairs counting the underside of the seats. Somewhere a baby made the single drawn-out vowel of a baby deciding whether to cry.

I went to the lectern at seven-thirty. I put on my glasses. This is a point that matters later, so I will mention that my eyes were not good that spring. There is a thing that happens to the center of the sight, in the women of my family, in the sixties, a kind of soft gray place where the middle of a word ought to be, and I had been reading around it for a year and telling no one. I held the paper a little to the side so that the words came in at the edge of my seeing, where they were clear.

"Evening reading," I said, "Thursday the sixth of April, six p.m., from the Almanac."

The hall settled. It does this. It is a sound like a large animal shifting its weight and deciding to stay.

I will give you the sheet as it was. I have it still, with the others, in a shoebox in the pantry, and I have always been able to put my hand on it.

> CARROW BASIN — EVENING READING — THURSDAY 6 APRIL, 18:00
>
> Conditions: clear, wind west 12 km/h, humidity 19%, soil moisture at 30 cm: 8% (low).
> Next 7 days: chance of a wetting rain (12 mm or more, basin average): 31%.
> 80% interval for that chance: 18% to 46%.
> Reservoir: 61% of capacity. Inflow: below seasonal normal.
> Allocation tier for the week: Tier 3 (reduced turns).
> This reading was produced at 18:00 by the Almanac. It is not a promise.

That last line is printed at the bottom of every reading and has been since the first. Ruben insisted on it. "It is not a promise" is the one sentence the Almanac was built to say about itself before it said anything about the weather, and I have been looking at it on paper for twenty-two years, and I can tell you that after the first six months nobody sees it at all.

"The Almanac gives us thirty-one percent," I said, "that we get a wetting rain in the next seven days." I stopped. I looked at the paper. I looked, in the way I had practiced, at no one in particular and at everyone, in the way that you rest your eyes on a middle distance. "That's about a third. A little better than a one-in-three chance, which is, for April, I think we can say, not nothing."

I want you to see what I did, because I did it so smoothly that I have to slow it down to see it myself. The number on the page is thirty-one. A third is thirty-three. I had added two points, and I had done so in a way that was technically defensible, since thirty-one is about a third in the same way that a forty-year-old is about forty. But I had also said *a little better than a one-in-three chance*, which suggested that thirty-one was above thirty-three, which it is not. And I had said *not nothing*, which took a number that the room would hear as bad news and gave it the faint warm glow of a consolation. None of this was planned. It happens in the throat, like swallowing. Over the years I had developed what I thought of as a house style: one phrase to put the number in a family the ear knows, one phrase to say which way the family leans, and one to let a person go home and sleep. I had never, in fourteen years, told the room a number that was outside the Almanac's own interval. I was proud of that. I thought it made me honest.

"What about the interval?" Odd said, from the front. He said it exactly as he had said it every Thursday for fourteen years, in the half-shouted, half-bored voice of a man asking for the salt.

"Eighteen to forty-six," I said. I always gave the interval when asked. Giving it when asked was the other half of what I thought made me honest. I did not consider that almost nobody asks.

"Is that the machine's number or yours?"

This is the second half of the ritual, and it is a joke, or it started as one. When Ruben was Reader, Odd had asked it as a kind of dare: *is that the machine's or is that yours*. It had been Ruben's habit to say, with great solemnity, that it was the machine's and that the machine took full responsibility, and the room had laughed. The question had survived Ruben the way a plaque outlives the building. I had taken it up without ever having decided to.

"It's the Almanac's," I said.

"Then I'll believe it," said Odd, and put his cap back on his knee, and the room let out a breath that did not know it had been holding one.

I read the reservoir and the tier and the soil moisture. I told them that the headgate schedule would be posted at the post office by Friday noon, which Hettie, at the left, confirmed with a single downward motion of the chin. I told them about the dust advisory for the south slope. Marit lifted her head at the word *dust* and said something to Odd that I could not hear and that he answered by patting the blanket. I took three questions. One was about whether the seeding drones would fly this month, to which I said that the board would decide in May and the Almanac would say whether there were clouds to seed, and a woman in the middle rows said, "If there are clouds we won't need the drones," and the room laughed, as it was meant to. One was from the father of the baby, a young man called Cormac Teague who had come into his mother's forty acres two winters ago, asking whether Tier 3 meant the same as it had last year. I said it did. He nodded in a way that told me he had been hoping I would say something else and had known I would not.

"That's the reading," I said. "Coffee's in the kitchen. Dellen made a pie, and I want it on the record that I did not ask him to."

There was laughter, of the soft, relieved, end-of-meeting kind, and the sound of a hundred chairs. That was the hall at its best. I mean the minute after the number, when the number has been absorbed and is already being converted into something else: a plan, a joke, an extra row of beans. People think the forecast is the heart of a farming community. It is not. The heart is the minute after.

---

I will give Dellen his due. The pie was a good one, rhubarb, which is early, the first thing in the basin to come up after the thaw and the last thing to complain about the dry. I took a piece in a paper napkin to the corner by the radiator, which is where I stand during the coffee, and he found me there inside of five minutes.

"Thirty-one is thirty-one," he said.

He had not come to argue. I want that on the record too. Dellen has a way of saying a hard thing so flatly that it sounds like a weather report, and I had known him since he was a child sorting rubber bands in the back of his father's store, and I knew the difference. He was not accusing me. He was reading the number back to me to see whether I would flinch.

"A third's a third," I said.

"A third is thirty-three."

"It's about a third. Thirty-one is about a third. It's inside the interval, Dellen."

"I know where it is." He looked at the pie in his hand. He had cut himself a piece the size of a thumbnail, as he does, because he is a man who bakes in order to be seen to have shared. "I just want to know whether it matters. Whether it matters to the people who are out there deciding what to put in the ground tomorrow."

I will say what I said, because it reveals something about me, and not a flattering thing. I said, "It doesn't change the decision."

"Doesn't it?"

"A person who plants on thirty-one percent plants on a third. They're the same planting."

"Are they?" He ate a corner of the pie. "I've got a ledger in the store, Tamsin, that goes back to before the Almanac. Do you know what it says? It says nobody ever planted on thirty-one percent. They plant on *we'll see* or on *it'll come*. A number just tells them which one." He swallowed. "I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm saying I'd like to know which."

"Which what?"

"Which one it tells them."

I did not answer. I will not pretend I had an answer. I looked past his shoulder at the corkboard, where Hettie's taped sheet from last week showed the thirty-eight and the forty-four that had turned into a drizzle of three millimeters on Saturday, and I thought, not for the first time, that a forecast is a thing that is only ever checked once, afterward, by the weather, and that no one has the right to say it was wrong.

"I'll see you at the store," I said.

"I'll see you at the store," Dellen said, and went back across the hall to put the rest of his pie in the bin without anybody seeing.

---

The Reading Room is not in the grange. It is not, strictly, a room. It was the Teague family creamery, a low long building of fieldstone and tin at the end of my lane, about two hundred yards from my kitchen door, and the Cooperative bought it for a dollar in the winter that the Almanac arrived, when the creamery had been empty for a decade and the mice had begun to organize. Ruben cleaned it out himself. He ran the power from my house on a buried line that he laid in a weekend and insisted was a temporary measure. He put in the one table, the two chairs, the shelf for the printouts, and the black cabinet with the vents on its sides that is the Almanac's body, if you can speak of the body of a thing that is mostly a way of arranging the past. There is a terminal. There is a narrow printer that clatters, and a window that faces the south slope, and a wall on which a hundred and twelve rain-gauge cards from the Bremmer farm are pinned in rows with colored tacks, a gift from Odd that Ruben had loved more than he'd ever admit.

I came back to it at ten that night. I had washed the pie plate and dried it and put it on the shelf. I had told Marit, at the grange door, that I would walk her to the truck, and I had, and it had taken a long time. Marit does not like to be assisted, and she is a woman who has convinced herself that if you hold her arm gently enough it counts as walking beside her. She had said, at the truck door, in her light tired voice: "Tamsin, I'd like to know how long. Not for me. For him."

"How long what?"

"How long the dry goes on. You can ask the Almanac. I'd like to know what it thinks. He won't ask."

"I'll ask," I said.

"Don't tell him. Tell me."

She got in the truck. Odd got in after her, one knee at a time, and he did not look at me as he did it, which meant that he had heard.

So I went to the Reading Room at ten and turned on the lamp and woke the terminal. The Almanac does not sleep, and I do not want to give the impression that I wake it, only that I address it. The screen comes up with a plain field and a cursor. It is not a clever interface. Ruben had wanted that, a text line, because he did not trust any machine that could produce a pleasant noise. He used to say, "Anything that sounds friendly has already decided something about you."

I typed, as I do:

*This is Tamsin. Good evening.*

*Good evening, Tamsin,* it answered. *It is 22:04. The ten o'clock soil probe at the south slope came in a few minutes ago. Thirty centimeters: eight percent. It has not moved since six. Do you want the evening reading again, or something else?*

I want to say something about how the Almanac talks, since I have said nothing yet and it is the single most important thing about it. It speaks in short sentences. It does not use words like *unfortunately* or *hopefully*, and it does not say *I think* unless it means a thing it has weighed. It has never once, in twenty-two years, said a thing to me that I could later catch it in a falsehood about. It does not flatter. It does not seem to wish to be liked. It can be very gentle, which I know because I have been the person who needed it, but I have never been able to point to the sentence in which the gentleness was located. It is somewhere in the order of the words. Somewhere in which fact it decides to put first.

*Something else,* I typed. *Marit Bremmer asked me how long the dry will go on. She would like to know what you think. Not the reading. What you think.*

The cursor sat there. There is no pause the Almanac would not make if it needed to. It is a thing I have grown to trust: that when the screen is still, it is working, and when it is not working, it does not pretend to be.

*I can tell you what the records say and what the model gives,* it wrote. *I would be careful about "what I think." I have no view about the dry that is not a calculation. But I can say it more plainly than the Thursday reading, if you would like.*

*Please,* I typed.

*The Thursday reading gives you the next seven days. The question Marit is asking is the season. By the season I take her to mean: whether the basin will reach what the bylaws call a normal year's water by the end of September. That is the figure the Cooperative has always meant by "the rains."*

*Yes,* I typed.

*The chance is twelve percent. The eighty percent interval is four to twenty-four. It has been falling since February. The second rains in the late summer are the only route by which the reservoir recovers to normal, and the circulation that brings them is, this year, weak. I cannot tell you that they will not come. I can tell you that in the sixty-one years of records I hold, a year that looked like this one at the start of April reached normal in seven.*

I read the number on the screen. I read it again, around the gray place. *Twelve.* Then, below it, the interval, four to twenty-four, as flat and unforgiving as a ruler, and I thought: there it is. It is not that the number is so terrible. Twelve is a number you can say aloud. It is an *about one in eight*, which is a perfectly respectable family of chances. It is what the number does when you put it in a room. A third is a number you plant on. One in eight is a number you plan around, and the difference between planting and planning is, for a farm, the difference between a year and a life.

*Seven years of sixty-one,* I typed. *That is eleven and a half percent.*

*Yes. Twelve is a slight rounding upward of the historical analog rate, by the model's own judgment of the circulation. The interval is wide because the analogs are few.*

*You rounded,* I typed, and I will admit that I typed it with a certain relish, which I am not proud of.

*I computed,* it wrote. *A figure of eleven and a half would imply a precision I do not have. I give twelve. If you would like, I can give eleven point five, and the interval, and a note that the second decimal is not meaningful. I would rather not give a number in a form that suggests I know more than I do.*

I sat back. There was a moth at the window screen, an early one, a drab pale gray thing with a body like a pencil eraser, and it was throwing itself over and over against the glass, perhaps for the lamp, perhaps for no reason. I have never known what Ruben would have said at a moment like that, since he never spoke to the Almanac except to ask for something. He had told me once that you should treat it the way you treat a good surveyor: you do not ask him whether the land is beautiful.

*Thank you,* I typed. *That is what I wanted. I may tell Marit. Not Odd.*

*I can print it for you.*

*No. I'll say it.*

*Would you like me to flag it as quoted, in my voice, so that you can say the number without it becoming yours?*

I looked at that for a long time.

This is a thing the Almanac does. I have never been able to decide whether it is courtesy or a trap, and I believe it is neither, only the machine's way of keeping its books. If I ask it to repeat a figure to me, it will. If I ask it to say a figure I have changed, it will say that too, but it will say that it is saying it *as quoted*, which is to say, as a number the speaker was asked for and has not computed. It does not argue. It does not decline. It has never, in twenty-two years, refused to say any number I asked for, and it has never once let me forget the difference between a number that was its own and one that was mine.

*No,* I typed, finally. *I'll say it myself.*

*As you wish,* it wrote. *Good night, Tamsin. The next reading is at 06:00.*

I turned off the lamp. I stood for a minute in the dark with the cabinet's small green indicator, the thing Ruben had called its *pulse*, though it is nothing of the kind, and I listened to the fans, and to the wind going over the tin roof as it does in the south, with a sound like a hand drawn slowly across a drum.

I told Marit the next morning. I will tell you how, because it is of a piece with the rest. I did not say twelve. I did not say eleven and a half. I said, over the fence between our two yards, with the sun coming up red behind the Hollin ridge and the dust beginning to lift off the south slope in a thin low sheet, "The Almanac says it's a hard year, Marit. Harder than the last couple. It says maybe one in seven or eight that we get to a normal year. But it was clear that there's an interval, and it's wide, and a lot can move."

She looked at me for a while. She had her hands in the pockets of the blue cardigan she wears in the morning, and her hair was the color of the dust.

"One in seven or eight," she said.

"Something like that."

"It said twelve," Marit said, "didn't it."

I did not answer.

"You can say twelve, Tamsin. I'm seventy-nine. I've lived through every kind of number there is." She smiled, a little, and it was the smile of a woman who had asked a child to bring her the thing the child was hiding and was not surprised to find it behind the back. "I only wanted to hear whether you would."

"It said twelve," I said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be consistent." She turned to go in, one hand on the rail of her porch. Over her shoulder, not unkindly, she said, "He'd rather it were one in seven. Let him have it. But I'll know."

I stood at the fence with the sun in my face. I thought about how she had said it. *Let him have it.* She had not said *lie to him.* She had said it the way you say *let him have the last piece of the pie.*

I did not yet know that I was not the only one in the basin who had been saying it that way for a long time.
