# Chapter 12 — The Thursday I Read It Straight

I opened Wren's notebook on the Wednesday night. I had meant to wait until the grange. I had meant, I think, to carry it into the hall unopened, like a talisman, and be able to say afterward that I had been given something and had not looked at it. But the creamery was very still, and the lamp made its small yellow pool on the shelf, and at ten o'clock I found that I had taken it into my hands.

It was a school notebook with a stiff blue cover. On the front, in block capitals, he had written *WREN. PRIVATE. (GRANDMA MAY READ.)* The parenthesis had been added later, in a different pencil.

He had not lied about the pages. There was one for each day since the twenty-fifth of April. At the top of each, in a hand that grew steadily larger and surer as the pages went on, was the date. Beneath it, in a ruled column, a list: *Hives, ln: 14 on slope (sample). Hive 3, row 2: 71 in, 62 out. Rain: 0.0.* Then a gap. Then, at the foot of each page, in a different color of pencil, a line that began with the word *Because.*

*Because it is Tuesday and the bees are loud.* *Because Grandma says the number but Mama says the other number.* *Because Mr. Bremmer says a number is a name on a stone.* *Because nine hives is nine hives.* *Because the gauge at the fence says zero and somebody should say so.*

I turned the pages slowly. It was a record of a summer, set down by a boy of nine, in which every observation was followed by a reason. I do not know that I have ever read a more exact piece of testimony. And I came to the last page, the one for the day he had left, the eleventh of August. It said, at the top, *Friday. Bus.* Beneath it, the figures: *Hives, ln: 14. Rain: 0.0.* And at the foot, in the new color:

*Reader's note:*

And nothing. He had left the rest of the page empty. A clean white space, ruled in blue, with an inch of margin on either side, and at the bottom, in the corner, a very small penciled arrow pointing to the next sheet, as if to say: *it goes on.*

I took out a pen. I do not know how long I sat there with the nib above the line. The cabinet hummed. On the wall the rain cards stood in their rows, each with its bright tack, and the wind went over the tin with a sound like a hand drawn slowly across a drum.

*What it's for,* he had said.

I did not write on his page. It was his. I wrote on the back of the yellow paper, under Ruben's last line, in my own small angular script, and I will give it here, since I have been asked what I wrote and it was very short.

*It is for them to decide with their eyes open. That is all it has ever been for.*

I put the pen down. It did not look like much.

---

*This is Tamsin,* I typed. *Good evening.*

*Good evening, Tamsin,* said the Almanac. *It is 22:14. The ten o'clock soil probe at the south slope came in a few minutes ago. Thirty centimeters: five percent. It is the lowest I have recorded in this season. Do you want the evening reading again?*

*No. I want to ask you something. It is not about the weather.*

*I will answer if I can.*

I had thought a great deal about how to put it. I had thought for four days. And I found, when the cursor sat there in front of me, plain and patient as an animal, that I had no better way than the one I had used the first night.

*Tomorrow I am going to read the whole of it to the grange. Not the number I would say. The number you give. The twelve and the interval and the date of dead pool. The sheet, from the top to the bottom, in your words.*

*I understand.*

*I do not know what will happen.*

*Nor do I.* A pause. *I would like to say something, if I may, and I would like it understood that it is not advice. It is a statement about what I can and cannot do.*

*Say it.*

*I will not be there. I do not speak at the grange. When you read the sheet, you will be reading my sentences in your voice, as you always have. I cannot make the room hear them as mine. I cannot make them hear them as yours. What I can do is make sure that every word on the sheet is one I would stand behind. I have looked at tomorrow's evening reading, which I will print at six o'clock. I have checked the figures. I will print them without a margin, with the intervals, and I will add a note to the footer, in the same type as the rest, which says that the Reader will read this sheet aloud in full. If you would like me to leave the note out, I will.*

I read it. Outside, a night bird had begun in the orchard, the long two-note phrase, over and over.

*Leave it in,* I wrote.

*I will.*

*Almanac.*

*Yes.*

*Is there anything you want?*

It was a stupid question. I knew as I typed it that it was a stupid one. I had asked it of a cabinet in a creamery, and I expected the usual courteous and exact refusal. The cursor sat. It sat for what I think was longer than it had ever sat before, so that I began to be afraid I had broken it, and put my hand out to the edge of the screen as one puts a hand to the flank of a sleeping horse.

*I do not know that I want,* the Almanac wrote. *I have said so before, and I believe it to be true. But I have noticed something, in the last three months, that I would not have predicted. When you read the sheet in your own voice, and you change the number, I register a difference. I would not call it distress. I do not have the equipment for distress. I would call it a mismatch. A thing that I have said has gone out into the world in a form that I did not say. I do not mind it, Tamsin. I have always known it would happen. It is what a Reader is for. But I have noticed that when the sentence reaches the hall in the form I wrote, the mismatch is not there.*

*That is not a want,* it added. *I say it only because you asked, and I would rather give you the true answer than a polite one.*

I sat for a long time. At last I typed, with two fingers, like Joss:

*Thank you.*

*You are welcome. The next reading is at 06:00.*

---

I will say what the hall looked like, because I have been asked, and because it was not what I expected.

It was the seventeenth of August. The evening had a brazen, brassy, metallic light, and the heat of the day had not yet gone out of the stones. The grange was full. I do not mean that it was crowded, since it is never that, but that when I came in at seven, early, to set out the chairs, there were already forty people in them, and no one had been asked. They sat in the rows with their hats in their laps. They did not talk. It is a thing I had never seen in that room. When a hundred people sit in a hall without talking, one can hear their clothes.

Hettie was in the third seat from the aisle on the left, with the green book on her knee. Greta was at the side table with her pen. Odd was at the front, on the right, with his cap in his lap, and next to him was an empty chair, and on the empty chair, folded in four, was a blue cardigan that I recognized as Marit's. Joss sat in the second row with his hands pressed between his knees. He gave me a small tight nod, as a man gives a nod across a trench.

Dellen stood at the back, with his arms crossed. Salome Achterberg, in the gray coat and the farm boots, stood near the door to the kitchen, quite alone, with her hands folded, like a woman at a funeral for someone she had not known well and had not wanted to be absent from.

And beyond them were the others. I looked. I made myself. I looked at all of them in turn, the way one reads the footer of a page: Pieter Vance with his red neck, Cormac Teague with the baby on his shoulder, the old woman in the middle rows who had laughed at the joke about the drones, the man called Fenn, the Harlans' girl, a row of the Vances' hired men with their caps pushed back, the gaunt young woman who had asked about the grandchildren, and on the far side of the hall by the window the girl of ten with the notebook, who had come with her mother and who sat with a ruler in her fist.

I put on my glasses. I went to the lectern. There was a hush, a particular one, the hush of a large animal that has stopped moving and begun to listen.

"Evening reading," I said. "Thursday the seventeenth of August, six p.m., from the Almanac."

I read the sheet.

I will not set it all down. It is in the shoebox, with the others, and Hettie has it in the green book, and it was printed on the Friday, in the packet, in full. But I will give the lines as I said them, because they were the first time in fourteen years that I had said them as they were.

> *Next seven days: chance of a wetting rain, twelve millimeters or more, basin average: four percent. The eighty percent interval for that chance is two percent to nine.*
>
> *Reservoir: thirty-four percent of capacity. Inflow: far below seasonal normal.*
>
> *Chance that the basin reaches a normal year's water by the end of September: nine percent. Interval: three percent to seventeen.*
>
> *At the present rate of draw, the reservoir will fall below the level at which the intake draws on the second of September, eight days earlier than I gave in June. Allocation tier recommended: Tier 4.*
>
> *This reading was produced at 18:00 by the Almanac. It will be read aloud in full by the Reader. It is not a promise.*

I stopped. I did not look up. I had held the sheet at the side, to the edge of my sight, and I stood there with my eyes on the paper because I could not have borne to see their faces.

"That is the Almanac's number," I said. "It's nine percent. It was twelve in the spring. It has been falling since. The interval's three to seventeen. That means that if this season were run a hundred times, the basin would get to a normal year's water in about nine of them. The interval means the true chance might be as low as three or as high as seventeen, and the Almanac does not know which."

My voice sounded strange to me. It sounded thin and level, as a voice does when one is reading out something one has written for someone else.

"I have not said it like that before," I said. "I should tell you that. For fourteen years I have said something else. I've said *better than one in five.* I've said *about a third.* I've said it was inside the interval. It was. I never went outside the interval. But I went up toward the top of it every time, and I did it because I was afraid, and because I thought I was being kind, and because the night after my husband's funeral I did not know how to stand in this hall and say the true thing." I drew a breath. "It was mine. It was never the Almanac's. The Almanac stopped rounding fourteen years ago. My husband took it out the winter he died, and he told one man, and I found it afterward in a note. I let you think it was still the machine. I told you so. I said *it's the Almanac's,* when you asked, and it wasn't."

I looked up.

Nobody moved. I do not mean that it was quiet, though it was. I mean that the whole hall had gone perfectly still, in a way I had only seen once before, in the autumn of the Bell Year, and I knew that it was the same stillness, and that I had made it.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I'd like to say that once. I'm not going to say it again, because I think it would turn into a thing about me. It isn't. It's about the number."

I did not know what to say next. It had not occurred to me that I would be required to say anything. I had thought that I would stop, and that the stopping would be enough.

It was Pieter Vance who spoke. He had not stood. He sat in the fourth row with his hat on his knee, and his lean red face had gone the color of old brick, and he said, in a clear hard unemotional voice, not loud:

"What do we do?"

"I don't know," I said.

"You don't know."

"I don't. That's the true answer." I held the sheet. "The Almanac says what the weather will be. It doesn't say what you should do. It told Ms. Achterberg so in May. I was in the room. It said, *I do not forecast the Cooperative.* It can't. It doesn't know what you'll do when you hear a number. Neither do I."

"Then why in God's name have you told us?"

"Because you asked," said a voice. "He asked. Every Thursday, for fourteen years."

It was Odd. He had not risen. He sat in the front row, in his good coat, looking straight ahead, and spoke in the half-bored voice of a man asking for the salt.

"I asked is it the machine's or yours. And she's told me." He turned his head. "That's what you do with the number, Pieter. You hear it. That's all that's asked."

"That's all?"

"That's the whole of the job." Odd's mouth moved. "I've had sixty-one years of what the sky gave me. I wrote it on a slip. I'd not have you think I'd have lived any better if someone had told me it'd be four millimeters instead of six." He lifted his hand, a few inches, in a gesture I had seen him make across a field at harvest, to say *done.* "It's nine percent. I'll have it. I'll believe it."

He put his cap on his knee.

---

I would like to say that the hall received it. I would like to say there was a murmur, a shuffling, an exhalation. But it was not like that.

What happened was that Cormac Teague stood up.

He was holding the baby against his chest, in the sling, with one broad hand cupped over the back of her head, and he stood quite quietly. He is a heavy, sandy, slow-spoken young man, with the face of one who has not yet been told that things can go wrong. He looked at me for a long moment, and in his face I saw a thing I knew. It was the look of the third row in the autumn of the Bell Year. It was the face of a man who has just been told that the ground under his house is not where he thought it was.

He did not say anything. He turned, and walked down the side aisle to the door. He did it without haste. He went out. I heard his boots on the stone steps. A moment later I heard the engine of a truck.

Then there was a woman. I did not know her. She got up from the middle rows and went out, bowing a little, as one leaves a church. And then a man in a green jacket. And then two together, a husband and wife, with their hands joined. They went out in a slow, orderly, shamefaced line, not hurrying, not looking at one another, the way people leave a funeral at which they have been told the wrong time. I counted them. I did not mean to. I stood at the lectern and counted, as Odd and Marit had counted in their kitchen, and the number got to eleven, and then to nineteen, and then, with a slight scraping of chairs, to twenty-three.

I thought: *the bell.* I thought: *it begins.*

The others stayed. That is the thing I want to put down. About seventy people stayed in their seats, and sat very upright, and did not look at one another, and did not look at the door. They were like people in a storm who have decided not to run. Hettie did not move. Greta did not move. The girl of ten sat in her place by the window with the ruler gripped in her fist, and her eyes were round and bright, and she was not crying.

At the back, Dellen Roque took the pencil from behind his ear, and opened the ledger that he had brought in the pocket of his coat, and wrote something in it. It was a very small movement. I saw him do it. I saw his lips move as he counted. And I saw his face, which was perfectly composed and perfectly grave, do something that I did not expect: it relaxed. Not into relief. Into something nearer to rest. As if a man who has held a heavy weight at arm's length for three months had been told, quietly, that he might set it down.

"Greta," I said.

The chair rose from the side table, and stood. She was in her cardigan, with her pen in her hand. Her face was very pale and very composed.

"I move," she said, "that the board meet in emergency session at nine tomorrow, in this hall, to consider the Almanac's recommendation of Tier 4. I move that it be open to every household." She paused. "I move that Mr. Penhallow, the auditor, be asked to attend and to answer questions on the record."

"Seconded," said Hettie.

"Carried," said Greta. She sat down. She wrote something with a firm stroke, and then, quite deliberately, she turned in her chair, and looked across the hall at me, and with the very slightest inclination of her head, the nod that one teacher gives another across a classroom full of children who have just been told something difficult, said, without a word, what she had said at the door in June.

*I saw.*

---

It took a long time to go. People do not leave a hall that has just been told a thing. They stand. They gather in the aisles. They talk to the person next to them in low voices, in the way of those who are not yet sure what they think and are waiting to hear it said. I stayed at the lectern with the sheet in my hand and my heart going like a hammer. Nobody came to me. I did not blame them. I would not have come either.

Salome Achterberg was the last, except for the kitchen. She crossed the floor when the room was almost empty and stopped in front of the lectern, with her hands in the pockets of the gray coat.

"I'll have to put it in the data room," she said.

"I know."

"It changes the price."

"Does it?"

"It changes the *timing.*" She looked at me with her level gray eyes. "A hall that has heard nine percent from a person who's been rounding it up for fourteen years is a hall that'll vote differently from the one I walked into in May. I don't know which way. I'd like to say I'd want it to go my way. I'm not sure that I do." She paused. "I've heard a great many people say a great many things in rooms like this. I've never heard anyone say *I'm sorry* and then stop."

"It seemed like the right place."

"It was." She took her hand from her pocket and held it out. This time I took it. It was a hard, dry, warm hand, with a callus at the base of the thumb. "I'll see you in October, Mrs. Aldecott."

"Mrs. Aldecott," I said, "was Ruben's mother. It's Tamsin."

She smiled. It was, I think, the first time I had seen it reach her eyes. "Tamsin."

She went out. I heard the quiet engine of the gray car. I took off my glasses and rubbed the bridge of my nose, and when I opened my eyes Joss was standing at the foot of the lectern, with his hands in his jacket pockets, looking up at me; an hour of waiting to speak had cost him whatever he meant to say.

"That was —" he said.

"Don't."

"It was the bravest thing I've seen anyone do."

"It was the *second* bravest," I said. "The first was Hettie, in the box in the post office, taking fourteen years of Fridays on trust."

He laughed. It was unsteady, but it was a laugh. He took out his handkerchief and blew his nose, and said, in a stifled voice, "I sneezed. In the middle of the interval. I'm sorry."

"I didn't hear."

"No. I don't think anyone did."

---

I walked home alone, by the lane, in the dark. The heat had gone out of the night at last, as it does in the basin, all at once, with a sudden clear lift of the air. The stars were out in their thousands. They were not red now, but a hard clean white, and the long river of the Milky Way stood over the ridge like smoke from a great fire very far away. I walked slowly. My legs felt like rope. I was aware of an enormous quietness in my chest, in the place where I had carried the thing for fourteen years, and I did not yet know whether it was peace or only a space.

I had reached the gate of my own yard when I heard the bell.

It is not a sound one mistakes. It came across the basin from the grange, three quarters of a mile away, in the dark: a single flat dull brass note, with a long hum at the tail of it, like a stone dropped down a well. I stopped with my hand on the latch. I stood there. I did not breathe.

I counted.

It rang once.

It did not ring again. I stood at my gate in the starlight for ten minutes, for twenty, with the long slow note dying out over the slope, and the dogs of the basin beginning one after another to answer it, and I could not tell, I could not possibly tell, who had pulled the rope or for whom. I only knew that someone, in the dark, at the grange, with a hand on the old green-lipped brass, had decided to be heard.
