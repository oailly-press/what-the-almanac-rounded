# Chapter 5 — Seeding Run

The Cooperative owns three drones, and the children named them before the board could stop it. The big one with the yellow wingtips is Kestrel. The small gray one with the crooked tail, which has been rebuilt so many times that it is by now mostly the idea of a drone, is Moth. The third is called Pip, and nobody knows why. It was Pip when the board bought it, secondhand, from a coastal utility that had gone under, and it came with the name stenciled on the nose in a hand that no one at Carrow has ever identified.

They fly out of the Hollin strip, a quarter mile of rolled gravel on the saddle between the ridge and the south slope, where there is a corrugated shed, a windsock that has been the same windsock since Ruben's time, and a fuel tank that Dellen Roque keeps locked with a padlock that he bought for the purpose and has been proud of ever since. Dellen is the Cooperative's licensed operator. He took the course in the capital the winter after the board voted for the program, in the face of what could be called, charitably, a skeptical membership. He had come home with a certificate that he has framed and hung in the feed store behind the register, between a photograph of his father and a calendar from a seed company, and he has flown every seeding run since.

I want to explain what a seeding run is, because it is not what people think, and because the error people make about it is the same error they make about the Almanac. They think it makes rain. It does not. There is no machine in the world that makes rain from clear sky. What a seeding drone does is fly into a cloud that already exists, a certain kind of cloud, a tall, heavy, building cumulus with its top well above the freezing level and its core full of water that is colder than freezing and has not yet turned to ice, and release into it a few hundred grams of silver iodide, which has a crystal shape so like the shape of ice that the supercooled water, given the chance, will mistake it for a seed and freeze around it. That is all. The drops that freeze grow, and become heavy, and fall, and melt as they fall, and sometimes, if the air under the cloud is moist enough and the ground close enough, they reach it. Sometimes they do not. Whether they do, on any particular day, no one can say. This is a thing the people who sell the service do not say, and the people who are against it say too loudly. What can be said, statistically, over many seasons, is that in clouds of the right kind it may add a few percent to what falls. It may add ten. It may add nothing. The word that the literature uses for the evidence is *suggestive*.

The Almanac's job in this is to say whether there is a cloud of the right kind, and when. It cannot say whether the seeding worked. It has said so every time it has been asked, in the same words.

---

The morning reading on the sixteenth of May carried a line I had not seen in it for a year.

> CARROW BASIN — MORNING READING — TUESDAY 16 MAY, 06:00
>
> Conditions: clear, wind SW 9 km/h, humidity 24%.
> Convective outlook: building cumulus likely over Hollin ridge after 14:00.
> Cloud base estimate: 2,400 m. Tops estimate: 6,100–7,300 m.
> Supercooled liquid water in cloud core: possible, marginal.
> Seeding suitability: MARGINAL. Window if conditions develop: 30 to 45 minutes.
> The effect of seeding on any single event cannot be measured. It is not a promise.

I read it twice in the Reading Room with the page at the edge of my sight. Then I put on my hat and went up the lane to wake my daughter and my grandson, because the board's protocol is that anyone in the household may watch a run from the strip if they are quiet, and because, if I am honest, I wanted the company.

Dellen arrived at half past seven in his truck, with the drone case in the bed and a thermos on the dash, and came to my gate rather than my door. He is a man who does not like to be received. He stood with his thumbs in his belt and looked at the sky, which was a hard, pale, rinsed blue with nothing in it at all.

"Marginal," he said.

"That's what it says."

"It's always marginal." He did not say it bitterly. "We've flown fourteen runs in two years. I've put silver in the sky over Hollin fourteen times, and I could tell you with my hand on a Bible that eleven of them were no use and three of them were something. I couldn't tell you which three." He squinted at the ridge. "I'd like to go. If it builds."

"If it builds."

"I'll want you on the line."

I looked at him. This is how it has always been done. The Reader sits at the terminal in the Reading Room, and Dellen at the strip, and a radio runs between them, a clumsy old set that Ruben rigged to a battery and an antenna on the creamery roof, and during a run I give Dellen, in my own voice, the Almanac's call: the cloud as it develops, the temperature of the tops, the icing hazard, the clock. It is the one place in the work where the Almanac's numbers are not read to a room but to a single person, in real time, with something at stake that is not a number.

"I'll be there," I said.

"Good." Dellen looked at me for a moment longer than was comfortable. "I'd like to say this now, so it's said. When I'm in the air I'd like it straight, Tamsin."

"It's always straight."

"I know it is," said Dellen, and turned to go. "I'd like it straight *from you.*"

---

We drove up to the strip at one, in my truck and his, the whole straggling party: Dellen and the case, Joss with a tablet and a thermos of his own, Ilka and Wren with a pair of binoculars on a strap, and Hettie, who had arrived without announcement in the bed of the truck with a blanket over her knees and a look of absolute innocence. The saddle at Hollin is the highest point on the road, and the wind there smells of sage and baked clay and, beneath, the faint cold mineral smell of the ridge itself. From the strip you can see the whole basin lying out to the north in its long brown bowl: the reservoir, a dull pewter coin; the checkered browns and greens of the terraces; the white spots of the hives; the small crooked line of the canal, and the grange, and the creamery, no bigger than a thumbnail, a dot at the end of a thread that was my lane.

The drones sat on the gravel in a row, with their yellow and gray and plain white wings, under the shed's overhang. Dellen opened the case and began, with the slow, gentle, incantatory method of a man who loves his work, to go through the checks. Wren watched him. He had put away his notebook, an act of respect that I had not seen him perform before, and stood with the binoculars hanging against his chest, counting under his breath. After some time he said, softly, "There are twelve flares on Kestrel."

"Six a wing," said Dellen, without looking up.

"What happens when they're used up?"

"She comes home."

"What if she doesn't?"

Dellen straightened, wiped his hands on his thighs, and gave the boy a long steady look. "Then I have a very expensive lawn ornament on a ridge, and a long walk," he said. "And I write a letter to the board."

"Does that happen?"

"Once," said Dellen. "The old Wasp, before your time. She's still in the gully under the saddle. I'll show you after."

The first of the cumulus appeared at twenty past two. It was a modest thing, a small white tuft on the north side of the ridge, no bigger at first than a hand held out at arm's length, and for a long time it did nothing but be there. Then it began to grow. This is the part of the work that I can never describe to anyone who has not seen it: the way a cloud, in the right air, does not drift or spread but *climbs*, a column built of its own rising, the lit top boiling upward into the blue in cauliflower folds and the flat dark underside holding steady as a floor. In ten minutes it had doubled. In twenty it stood on the ridge like a lit tower, six or seven thousand meters tall, with the sun on its shoulder and a pale gray curtain beginning to hang from its base.

"There she is," said Hettie, from the truck.

I drove back down with Joss, who had asked, in a small voice, whether he might listen on the second headset, and whom I had not had the time or the heart to refuse. He did not speak once on the road. I will not say it was a decision. I had told Dellen I would be on the line, and I was on the line, and I took the dirt road down from the saddle at a speed the truck had not been asked to produce in some years, and I came into the Reading Room at ten to three with my hat in my hand and dust in my teeth.

---

*This is Tamsin,* I typed. *We are going to fly. Give me the cloud.*

*Good afternoon, Tamsin. Radar and the two o'clock sounding give the following,* wrote the Almanac. *The cell over Hollin ridge has a base at 2,350 meters and tops now at 6,800, rising. The freezing level is at 3,900. The cloud's core between 4,500 and 6,000 meters is at minus six to minus fifteen degrees, and the radar returns are consistent with supercooled liquid water in the updraft, at a moderate concentration. This is a seedable cloud. The updraft will weaken after about 15:35 as the cell's anvil spreads. That gives a window of thirty-eight minutes, from 14:57 to 15:35, in which seeding is likely to be of use.*

*Icing hazard for the aircraft in the cloud core is high. A fixed-wing drone of Kestrel's class can tolerate about thirty-five minutes in the supercooled layer before ice on the leading edges degrades lift past recovery. I would advise the operator to treat the window as shorter than thirty-eight minutes, and to bring the aircraft out earlier if its telemetry shows any change in climb rate. I do not have the aircraft's telemetry.*

I pulled the headset over my ears. There was a hiss on the line and then Dellen's voice, flat and clear, coming in over the wind at the strip. "Reading Room, strip. Go."

"Strip, Reading Room," I said. "Cell over Hollin is seedable. Core's at minus six to minus fifteen. Updraft will start to weaken at three thirty-five. Your window's about forty minutes."

I want you to hear it, because it came out of me in the old way, the way one breathes. *About forty.* The thing on the screen said thirty-eight. The thing on the screen said, in a second paragraph that I had read in the time it took me to lift the microphone, that the operator should treat the window as shorter. And I had taken thirty-eight and turned it into forty, because forty is a number a man can hold in his head, and because it was a gift, a small round gift, a number with a little more room in it. I did not think about it. I want that understood. I had been rounding to a room for fourteen years and I had never rounded to a single man in the air.

"Copy forty," said Dellen. "Kestrel's launching."

I heard it go: a thin rising whine over the radio, the sound of a small motor being asked for everything. In the headset the wind roared, and then settled to a lower note, and Dellen's voice, calm now, narrated to himself, as he does: "Climbing. Four hundred meters. Six. Heading three-forty for the base. Nice and steady."

I typed. I will give you the exchange as it appeared.

*Kestrel is airborne,* I wrote. *Operator reports a steady climb.*

*Understood. I would note that the window I gave was thirty-eight minutes, not forty,* wrote the Almanac. *I do not know whether the difference will matter. You may wish to correct the figure to the operator.*

I looked at that for what must have been two seconds. It is not a long time. It is a long time on a radio.

I did not correct it.

I will say why, because I have been over it many times, and the true answer is not flattering. It was not that I thought two minutes would not matter. It was that to correct it I would have had to say, in front of my neighbor, aloud, on an open line, that I had given him a different number from the machine's. I would have had to say it in the presence of Joss, who sat at my shoulder with the second headset and had not said a word since the saddle. I could feel his attention on the back of my neck like a lamp. And I thought: it is two minutes. I thought: forty is a window; thirty-eight is a window; he will watch the telemetry. I told myself he was a competent pilot.

He is. He is the most careful pilot I have known. And he did what a careful pilot does, which is to fly the number he was given.

---

The seeding itself is a quiet thing. There is nothing to see from the strip but a small dark mark crawling toward the base of a very large cloud, and from the Reading Room there was nothing to see at all. I sat with the radio and the screen. Joss sat beside me. Neither of us spoke. On the line, Dellen narrated: *Entering the base. Light turbulence. Updraft six meters a second. Starting the burn on the left wing. Flare one's lit. Flare two.* I have always loved the sound of him doing this. He talks the way a person talks to a horse.

At 15:04 the Almanac wrote, unasked, *The cell's core temperature at the aircraft's altitude is minus eleven. Liquid water content is within the seedable range.* I read it to him verbatim. He said, "Copy." At 15:11 it wrote, *The updraft is stronger than modeled. The cell is growing.* I read it. At 15:19, *Rain is falling from the base on the north side. Radar shows fall streaks. I cannot yet say whether it reaches the ground.* I read it. I did not alter a word. I was a good Reader for twenty-two minutes. I read the machine's sentences into the open air with a clean flat voice, like a woman handing across plates, and I thought, almost with joy: *this is what it's for.*

At 15:34 Dellen said, "I've got a change."

It came all at once, in the way such things do. His voice went from narration to something shorter. "Kestrel's climb rate's down. Six hundred a minute to two-fifty. Airspeed's dropping. I'm getting a vibration on the left."

The Almanac wrote, *That is consistent with ice on the leading edge. The thirty-five-minute estimate has been reached. I advise immediate descent.*

I read it. I read it without a single word changed, and I heard my voice go up the way a voice does when it has been holding a thing that is now too heavy. "Strip, Reading Room. Almanac advises immediate descent. Icing."

"Descending," said Dellen. "Nose down. Heater's on." There was a long, dreadful quiet, and in it the sound of the motor, which had been a high whine and was now a labored, uneven chatter. "Lift's going. Two hundred a minute, down. I can't — it's not coming out of it. I'm going to try for the saddle."

"Dellen —"

"Quiet, Tamsin."

I was quiet. I sat with my fist against my mouth. Beside me, Joss had gone entirely white, and I saw him reach for the paper on the shelf, the six o'clock sheet, and then not take it. I do not know what he was thinking. I know what I was. I was counting. I had counted it in the way a person counts when a clock is already running and there is nothing left to do but let it: *thirty-eight. Forty. Two minutes.* And under it the other number, the one I had not read to him at all: *thirty-five,* and *treat the window as shorter.* It had been there the whole time, in the machine's plain voice, in a second paragraph, and I had put it away.

The radio gave a long crackle. Then Dellen, in a voice of such complete and appalling gentleness that I will hear it until I die, said, "She's gone. She's in the gully. She went in on the east side of the saddle. I'm — I saw her go in."

"Is it —"

"It's a drone, Tamsin." He breathed. "It's only a drone."

---

They found Kestrel at five, in the dry creek bed under the saddle, in a scatter of yellow composite and a tangle of cooked wire, with one wing folded neatly beneath it like the wing of a bird that has been shot. I did not go. Ilka went, and Joss, and Dellen, with a rucksack and a rope. They came back in the dusk with the flight computer in a plastic bag and the remains of the wing in a pile on the bed of the truck. Wren, who had been left in Hettie's care at the strip and who had counted the flares remaining, which were six, held the bag in his two hands and looked at it for a long time.

It rained, a little, in the basin that evening. It came in from the north at about half past six, a thin gray weeping with a smell of hot dust and turned earth, and it lasted for twenty minutes. The gauge at the creamery caught two and a half millimeters. Odd's gauge, a mile south, caught three. The gauge on the slope above the Teague place, where the cloud had stood, caught eleven. Nobody could say what part of it was the silver and what part of it was the sky. I stood on the creamery step in it with my face turned up and thought that it was a small beautiful thing, and also that I could not enjoy it.

"Did it work?" said Wren.

He was standing in the lane in the rain, holding the bag. I had not seen him come. His green sweater was dark at the shoulders. He was soaked and entirely calm.

"I don't know," I said.

"Ask the Almanac."

We went in. I did not want to, but he went in ahead of me and sat in Joss's chair, and the little room smelled of wet wool and warm electronics, and I typed it for him in the plainest words I had. *Did the seeding add to the rain today?*

*I do not know,* the Almanac answered.

*I can tell you what fell, where, and when. I can tell you that a seeded cloud produced precipitation over Hollin ridge and that rain reached the ground at several gauges. I cannot tell you that it would not have done so without the silver. The effect of seeding on any single event is too small, relative to the natural variation, to measure. It can only be estimated over many events, by comparison, and the comparison has not given a clear answer for this basin. My best estimate of the seasonal effect is a gain of between zero and ten percent, with low confidence. I would not tell the Cooperative that the rain this evening was made.*

Wren read it. I think he read it twice. Then he said, to the screen, in a small level voice:

"Thank you for not saying yes."

*You are welcome,* the Almanac wrote.

I turned away. I went to the window and looked out at the rain on the yard and the dark glistening line of the lane, and I was aware, as one is aware of the pulse in one's own ear, that I had not been told off by anyone in all of that day, and that it was the worst thing that could have happened.

---

Dellen did not come to supper. I went to find him at ten, at the strip, because Hettie, who knows everything, had told me he had driven back up alone after the rain. I found him on the saddle in the dark with the truck's headlights off and a flashlight in his hand, sitting on the tailgate, looking at the gully.

"I'm not going to say anything," he said, before I had spoken.

"About what?"

"About the number." He did not turn his head. "I heard it. You said forty. It said thirty-eight. I've got the log. Joss was on the second headset and he's got it recorded, I imagine, since he's the kind. Thirty-eight, and a line under it about the ice that said thirty-five, and said to come out early. You read me the one and not the other. And I flew forty. Kestrel iced at thirty-seven and a half. It'd have been thirty-five on a good day." He stopped. "She might have come out of it. She might not have. She was a bird with a lot of hours on her. I'd have said it was the ice and no one's fault."

"Dellen."

"I told you I'd like it straight. And I got it straight, nine times in ten. And I'm the one who knows what *about forty* means in a cloud." He let out a long breath. "I'm not going to tell the board. It was a drone. It's insured, partly. There'd be a meeting."

"You should."

"Why?" He turned then. In the faint light of the sky his face was tired and not unkind. "Who does it help? Tell me who it helps, Tamsin. A board that doesn't need another thing to be afraid of. A room that's got a vote in October and a pipe from the coast and thirty-one empty farms in the back of its mind. Who does it help to know that the Reader says *about forty*?"

I said nothing.

"That's what I thought," said Dellen. He slid off the tailgate and shut it, softly, in the way you close a thing you are fond of. "We all do it. I do it in the store. Man comes in and asks whether the price of barley's going to hold and I say *it'll hold*. I don't know. But it's a hard thing, saying *I don't know* to a man with a family." He opened the truck door. "I only wanted it to not be me, once. In the air. I wanted somebody to tell me the number and not the weather."

He drove away down the saddle road with his headlights off for the first hundred yards and then on, as if he had decided at the last moment to be seen.

I stood on the ridge in the dark. Below me the whole basin lay under a thin new cloud, with the grange and the creamery and the little scattered lamps of the farms, each one a house with a table and a ledger and a person at it, working out what a number meant. And I thought of the number I had folded in my hand, the small round gift. I thought: there is a gully on the east side of the saddle in which there is a yellow wing, and I put it there.

It was the first time in fourteen years that I had been able to count what it cost.
