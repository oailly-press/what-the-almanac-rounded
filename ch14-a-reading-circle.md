# Chapter 14 — A Reading Circle

The board decided on the fourteenth of September, at a meeting that lasted forty minutes, and it decided, as boards do, against both petitions and for something that neither of them had thought of.

I will give the minute as Hettie wrote it. It is in the green book, in her small upright hand, under the date, between a note on the Tern Creek headgate and a resolution about the stove.

*Moved (Lindqvist), seconded (Fenn): that the office of Reader be retained and made plural. That the evening sheet be read at every Thursday meeting in full and without alteration by a Reader drawn by rota from the membership, who shall have been heard to read it twice in practice. That the Reader of the evening shall, after the sheet, add a Note, which shall be in the Reader's own words and which shall begin with the words "This is mine." That the present Reader (T. Aldecott) be named Reader of Record, to train the rota, to keep the Reading Room, and to answer to the board. Carried, 8 to 1. Mr. Vance dissenting, on the ground that "somebody will have to do it properly."*

Beneath it, in the margin, in pencil, as a thing she had apparently not been able to leave alone, Hettie had written: *He volunteered the same night.*

---

I had not meant it to be a ceremony. I have a horror of ceremonies. But I think that what the board had done, without knowing it, was to make the thing that Wren had tried to say in his notebook into a rule. *This is mine.* It was only four words. It was, I think, the first time in the history of the Cooperative that anyone had been required, as a matter of procedure, to say which of the things they were saying were their own.

It changed the sound of the hall. I will try to describe it. In the old way, when I stood at the lectern, there was one voice, and it was a blend, a single stream in which the Almanac's sentences and mine ran together so that the listener could not have said where one ended. Now there were two, and the seam between them was audible. The Reader would read the sheet in a flat voice, as one reads a document. *Next seven days: chance of a wetting rain: four percent.* There would be a very short pause. And then the Reader would say, in a slightly different voice, a warmer and more uncertain one, a voice that had been brought out of a pocket, *This is mine.* And then whatever she thought.

It is a strange discipline. I have trained a good many readers now, and I have seen what it does. It does not make people honest. It makes them *notice.* It gives them a small handle on their own sentences, so that they can feel the moment, in the middle of saying *it'll be all right,* at which the thing turns from a measurement into a wish. They do not always stop. But they hear it. And once a person has heard it, I think, she is not able to un-hear it for the rest of her life.

---

We began the training on the Saturday, the sixteenth, in the grange, at two in the afternoon, with the shutters closed against the heat.

Hettie had made a list. There were ten names on it, drawn from a hat, which she had held out at arm's length to the whole Cooperative on the Thursday, so that anyone could see it was not rigged. They stood in a ragged row on the stage in front of the lectern, with the look of a group of people who had been told they were going to be photographed. There was Greta, who had insisted that the chair should not be exempt. There was Hettie herself, on the principle, she said, that a person who has sat in the third seat for forty years has earned the right to find out how it feels from the other side. There was Cormac Teague, with the baby, Gwen, on his shoulder. There was Brigid. There was a heavy slow man named Harlan who had not spoken at a meeting in a decade, and the gaunt young woman who had asked about the grandchildren, whose name, I learned, was Sunniva. There was the girl of ten, whose name was Maud, who had been drawn by the hat and whose mother had said that she might. There was Dellen. There was Joss, who had been drawn despite not being a member, on the ground, Greta ruled, that the hat did not care.

And at the end of the row, upright and ferocious, in his shirtsleeves, with his arms folded, stood Pieter Vance.

"I'll go first," said Pieter.

"You don't have to go first."

"I'd like to. I'd like to see how it's done." He unfolded his arms. "I've said what I think of it. I'd like to say it in the right voice."

I gave him the sheet. It was the evening sheet for the sixteenth. He took it in both hands, as a man takes a summons, and stepped to the lectern, and looked at the empty hall for a moment with his lean red face, and I saw him draw a breath, and square his shoulders, and lift the paper to the level of his eyes.

I do not think I have ever heard it read better.

He read it in a voice like a gate being opened. It was flat, hard, deliberate, without a particle of ornament, and each number fell into the hall like a stone into a well: *Chance of a wetting rain in the next seven days. Six percent.* A pause. *Interval. Three to eleven.* A pause. *Reservoir. Twenty-nine percent. Tier four.* He did not hurry, and he did not slow. He gave the weight of each figure exactly its weight, as a butcher gives weight, and when he came to the last line he read that too, *It is not a promise,* in the same voice, with the same exactness, and it rang. It rang in a way that I had not heard in twenty-two years of hearing it said.

Then he stopped. He lowered the paper. He looked out at us, and for a long moment I thought that he was not going to say it, that the four words would stick in him like a bone.

"This is mine," said Pieter Vance.

"Go on," I said, very softly.

"I think —" He stopped. He was sweating, I saw. He wiped his upper lip with the back of his hand. "I think it'll not rain. I think we'll get to October and the reservoir'll be at twenty and we'll have the vote. And I think it'll go through. I don't want it to. I'd not say what I want. But I think it will."

"Is that all?"

"No." He looked at the paper. "I'm afraid. I'll say that. I put in barley in a year I shouldn't have, and I've not forgotten what that was, and I don't want to do it again. I'd sooner be told. But I'm afraid of being *told.*" His voice cracked, not much, a hairline. "That's mine."

He stepped back from the lectern. Nobody spoke. I saw Greta put her hand over her mouth, and Dellen, behind her, looking at the floor.

"Was that right?" said Pieter, sharply. "Did I do it?"

"Yes," I said.

"It felt like being skinned."

"It does at first."

"Does it stop?"

"It gets to be like a coat," said Odd.

He had come in without being seen. I do not know how long he had been standing in the door at the back, in the canvas coat, with his cap in his hand, but he came down the aisle now with his slow, careful, rolling walk, and the whole row on the stage turned to watch him. He looked terrible. He had lost a stone since the funeral and his face had gone to bone, with the skin drawn over it like paper over a lamp. But he held his head up. He came as far as the stage and stood at the foot of it with one hand on the rail.

"You've got the voice for it," he said to Pieter. "I'll say that. I never thought. All these years I've sat in the front row listening to a woman who couldn't help being kind, and I'd not have said you could be the other. The exact one." He nodded slowly. "It's wanted."

"Thank you," said Pieter, stiffly.

"I'd like to coach," said Odd. "If I may."

I looked at him. He had never asked for anything in his life. "Odd, you're not well —"

"I'm not dead," said Odd. "I'd like to be useful. I've asked the question every Thursday for fourteen years. I think I'd know what the answer should sound like." He turned his washed-out eyes on the row. "I'll tell you the first thing. You don't say *about.* That's the whole of it. Say *nine.* If it's nine, say nine. You can say *and the interval's three to seventeen.* You can say *I'd not bet on it.* But you say nine. A person can stand on a nine. A person can't stand on an *about.*"

"What about *around?*" said Harlan.

"No."

"Roughly?"

"I'll hit you," said Odd, mildly, and Hettie began to laugh.

---

It went on until the vote, the better part of three weeks. I would not have believed that a rota of ten ordinary people reading a sheet of paper aloud could be a form of entertainment, but the news got round, and by the third Saturday there were forty or fifty in the hall, sitting at the back in the half-light with their sandwiches, and when it was Dellen's turn they applauded.

He had been dreading it. I knew, because he had asked me, in the aisle, twice, in a low voice, whether it was permitted to read the sheet in the third person. It was not. He read it in a clean careful feed-store voice, giving each figure its due, with a slight rising inflection at the end of the interval, as if he were reading out a price. When he came to *This is mine,* he stopped. He stood there for a very long moment, a big balding man in a leather apron at a lectern that had been made from a sideboard, with the whole hall's worth of silence in the room.

"I've got a ledger," said Dellen. "I gave it to Hettie. It was a — I'd been counting who'd vote to sell. I stopped." He looked at the paper. "This is mine. I don't know what I'm going to vote. I've never not known. I've always known what the barley would do. But I keep thinking about my father, who sat in that feed store in the Bell Year and watched the bell go, thirty-one times, and said to me, across the counter, *it's the number that does it.* And I've spent my whole life thinking he was right. And I'm not sure he was. I think it was the *bank.* I think the number only *looked* like it." He wiped his face. "That's mine. I don't know if it's useful."

"It's useful," said Odd, from the back.

The girl, Maud, read the second sheet. It was the one the Almanac had begun to print each week, *what would change the number,* and she stood on a box that Hettie had fetched from the kitchen, so that her chin cleared the lectern, and read it in a high clear singsong, from beginning to end, with a tremendous solemnity, without once looking up. *A southward dip in the upper-level flow over the coast. A dew point at Hollin ridge above twelve degrees for two mornings together. The bees are low.* She read the last one with particular feeling. And then she said, in exactly the voice she had been taught, "This is mine," and stopped, and looked at the paper, and I saw her mouth work.

"It's eleven," she said. "Yesterday and today. I've got the pencil on the string. Eleven, and eleven, and a half." She looked up. Her eyes were enormous. "It's not twelve. It's close. I'm — that's mine. I think it's close."

Nobody laughed. There was not a single person in the hall who laughed.

---

Salome Achterberg came to the orchard on the twenty-second.

I had not expected her. I had thought that she would stay away, as a person stays away from a house in which there has been a death, and I had been, to be honest, relieved. But I came out of the bean row at four o'clock with a trug of the last scarlet runners and saw the gray car standing at the foot of the lane, and her, a little way along under the old pear tree, with her hands in the pockets of the gray coat, looking up into the branches.

"I'd have knocked," she said. "I wasn't sure."

"It's an orchard. It's anyone's."

"It's yours." She took her hands from her pockets. "I wanted to speak to you in the open. Before I go to the grange. I'd like it to be something you've heard first from me, with no one watching. I think it's only fair."

I put down the trug. I sat on the low stone wall under the tree. She did not sit. She stood with the light coming down through the leaves on her gray head, in the old boots, and I saw that she had been up late. There was a small tremor at the corner of one eye.

"I took your reading to my board," she said. "The seventeenth of August. I sent the whole thing. Joss's report. Your statement. The sheet. I told them what I'd heard, and what I thought it did to the price." She breathed. "They didn't like it. It's — you'll understand. A company that's about to pay fifty-eight million dollars for the water rights of a basin likes to know that the basin's going to be afraid. It's a thing that makes a price. And what I'd told them was that for the first time in sixteen years the basin had *heard* it, and I didn't know what it would do."

"They'd rather you'd said it'd be afraid."

"They'd rather I'd said anything." She gave a tight smile. "I said I didn't know. It's not an answer they're used to."

"What did they do?"

"They told me to think about it," said Salome. "And I did. I thought about it for a month. I went home to the flat. I took my father's gauge out of the drawer." She stopped. "I read it. All of it. The first time. I'd not had the nerve."

"What did it say?"

"It said he'd been right about everything for seventy-one years and nobody had listened." She laughed, once, shortly. "I mean he'd been wrong sometimes, but he'd *said* when he was wrong. He wrote the word beside the number. *Hail.* *Late.* *Dry.* I'd never known he kept the words." She looked at me. "I thought, I've been going into rooms like yours for twenty years with a number, and I've never once been asked what it was *for.*"

I did not say that the same thing had happened to me. I did not need to.

"I went back to my board," she said, "last Tuesday. With a different offer." She took a folded paper from her coat. "I'd like to read it to you. Not as the company's. As mine. I've put it in writing. It's here, on the top, in my own hand: *This is mine.*"

She read it standing, in a clear low even voice, and I listened with the last of the sun on my hands.

It was not a purchase. That was the first thing. Meridian would not buy the Cooperative's rights. It would, instead, enter into a fifteen-year agreement to lease a share of the basin's *surplus,* the water in the wet years, the high flows of the good springs, which the Cooperative could not at present store and which ran away down the creek to the sea. The Cooperative would build, with Meridian's money, a second reservoir on the upper terrace at the Fenn place, to catch it. In the dry years there would be nothing to lease, and Meridian would pay nothing and take nothing. In the wet years Meridian would take forty percent of what was caught, at a fixed price, and the rest would stay.

And there was a second part. A *fallowing program.* Any household that chose, voluntarily, to take a third of its irrigated land out of production for ten years would be paid, per acre, a sum that Salome had worked out in a hurry on the back of an envelope and that was, she said, almost certainly too low. The water saved would not be taken. It would be left in the reservoir. It would be left, she said, for the Cooperative to hold against exactly the year that this was.

"It's not enough," said Salome, folding the paper. "I know that. It isn't two hundred and seventy thousand dollars a household. It doesn't solve the thing your daughter's numbers say. It doesn't save the basin from what's coming in fifteen years. But it's the best I can do that doesn't buy you out. And I think —" She hesitated. It was the first time I had seen her hesitate. "I think it might be the thing a person would build if she'd listened to what you read."

"Will your board agree?"

"I don't know."

"Will you fight for it?"

"I've already lost an argument with them to bring it here," said Salome. "I'll lose another one in October, if it comes to it." She slid the paper back into the pocket of her coat. "I'm fifty-four. I've got a pension. I'd rather be the person who tried."

I sat under the pear tree and looked at her, a tall gray woman in old boots, with her father's gauge somewhere behind her eyes. I thought of the day that I had first seen her get out of the car against the long orange light. I had taken her for an enemy. I realized that I had been wrong in a way that was almost exactly like the way that I had been wrong about myself.

"Read it to the hall," I said. "Not me. Them. On the fifth of October. At the vote."

"That's what I came to ask."

"Straight. The way you did in May. Don't round it."

"I wouldn't know how," said Salome Achterberg, and for the first time in my presence she laughed, a low, open, startled laugh, and the sound went up into the pear tree and a pair of sparrows flew out.

---

Ilka called on the twenty-eighth. It was a Thursday, late, after the reading. The old wall telephone, with its cord, rang in the empty kitchen, and I stood for a moment with my hand on the receiver and knew who it was by the quality of the silence.

"Mama."

"Ilka."

"I've been reading the sheets." Her voice was low, and thick, as if she had been crying or had been asleep. "Hettie sends them. She's sending two now. The one with the table and the one that says *what would change the number.*" She stopped. "I've never seen a forecast do that."

"No."

"It's — in my entire career I've never once seen a model give you the conditions under which it'd be wrong. They give you the interval. They give you the scenario. But the *signs.* The things you'd look out of the window for." She drew a breath. "It's *so* simple. It's the thing a farmer'd want. It's the thing my professors told me was impossible. And a thing in a shed in Carrow's been capable of it for twenty-two years."

"It was asked."

"It was asked." She laughed, unsteadily. "That's what I keep coming back to. It was only ever waiting for someone to ask."

I did not answer. I stood with the cord wrapped around my finger and listened to my daughter breathing a hundred and forty kilometers away.

"I'm not coming round," said Ilka. "I want to be clear. I think the sale's still right. I think the water balance is still the water balance. I think in fifteen years —" She stopped. "But I think I've been wrong about something. I've been wrong that the problem was the *number.* I thought if you gave people the true number they'd see what I saw. And I think the problem was that nobody ever told them what to *do* with it."

"That's what Brigid Teague said."

"Who?"

"A woman with a six-month-old baby," I said, "in a creamery."

"Right." She was quiet for a time. "I'll be back on the first. I'll be there on the fifth, on the side I told you."

"I know."

"Mama." Her voice dropped. "Is Odd all right?"

I looked out the window. The light had gone, and the lane lay pale in the dark, running down to the creamery, where one small window still burned. Beyond it, on the slope, a long way off, I could see a single moving light, a lantern, going slowly along a row of the rye, and stopping, and going on.

"He's out with the gauge," I said. "He goes every night now. He says he wants to be sure."

"Of what?"

"I don't know," I said. "He didn't say. I think it's only that he'd like to be there."
