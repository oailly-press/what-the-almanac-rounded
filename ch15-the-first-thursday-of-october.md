# Chapter 15 — The First Thursday of October

Odd read the sheet.

I had not known he would. Nobody had. The rota for the fifth of October had been drawn in September, and the name that came out of the hat on that occasion was Harlan's, the heavy slow man who had not spoken at a meeting in a decade and had, to everyone's surprise, turned out to read like a priest. But on the Tuesday before the vote he came to the post office with his cap in his hands and told Hettie that he would be obliged if he might give up his place. His wife was ill. He was not himself. He would give it to a better man, and he had asked, and the better man had said yes.

"Who?" said Hettie.

"Bremmer."

"Odd doesn't read."

"He's asked." Harlan turned the cap. "He came to the house. He said he'd been asking the question for fourteen years and he'd like to know what it was to be asked. He said he'd stand."

I heard this on the Wednesday, from Hettie, over the post-office counter, among the stamps and the string, and for a moment I could not answer. It was the day before the vote. I had been in the creamery since six, with the Almanac's draft of the evening sheet, working through it with the slow dull care of a woman who has been told that something is going to be heavy and has begun, without wishing to, to carry it early.

"He's not well," I said.

"He's dying," said Hettie. She said it flatly, without any emphasis, in the voice of a woman reporting a price. "I've known since the funeral. So do you. He knows. He'd like to do it before." She turned a stamp over with her thumbnail. "I thought you'd want to be told."

---

Ilka and Wren had come back on the Sunday.

I will not say that it was easy. It was not. We had a supper on the Sunday night, the three of us, at the long table, with a stew that I had made and that nobody ate much of, and a silence between my daughter and me that was less like a wall than like a long pane of glass, in which each could see the other quite clearly and could not touch. She had grown thinner. She wore the field jacket. She did not mention the contract, or Meridian, or the sale. She asked how the bees were, and I said that I had a hundred and one, and she said that she was sorry about the nine, and I said that Wren had counted them, and she looked at him, and he looked at his stew.

It was Wren who took the glass out of it. He had come back with the same green sweater and a new notebook and a very serious expression, and he had spent most of the evening, I noticed, studying me. At last, over the pudding, he said in a clear, level, conversational voice:

"Are you going to be on the sheet?"

"What sheet?"

"The one with the names. For the Circle. Maud says there's a rota. She said you aren't on it, because you're Reader of Record."

"That's right."

"That's silly," said Wren. "You should read at least once. You know how." He considered. "You could do the Note."

"I don't think —"

"I think you should." He scraped the last of the pudding. "Mama's going to vote for the sale. She told me on the bus. I said, *that's all right, it's your vote.* But I told her it'd be a mistake if she thought Grandma was the only one who's ever rounded." He frowned. "I said everyone does it. I said it's a thing people do for each other. But you have to say."

Ilka had stopped with her spoon in the air.

"I said it badly," said Wren. "But I think you both know what I mean."

I do not know what my daughter would have said. I know only that she put down her spoon, with great care, and looked at her son, and then she looked at me, and for the first time in two months there was something in her face that I recognized. It was the look that she had worn at the age of seven, when she had been told by her father that the cat had gone to live on a farm.

"We know what you mean," she said.

---

The grange on the fifth of October held, by Hettie's count, two hundred and nine people, which is more than it was built for by a margin that the fire code would have called a crime. They stood in the aisles and along the walls. They sat on the stage and on the window ledges, and the children were on their parents' shoulders, and the heat of so many bodies had made the radiators, for the first time that autumn, quite unnecessary. It was a cold night. There was a clear hard glittering sky outside, and the first frost of the year was forming on the road, and the great double doors had been left open so that those who could not get in could stand on the porch in their coats and hear.

The ballot box stood on the table at the front. It is an old walnut box with a brass slot and a brass lock, which Ruben made for the Cooperative's first vote and which has stood since in the cupboard under the stairs. Greta had polished it. She had set it on a white cloth, with two ballot slips for each household, one white and one blue, and a basket of pencils. She had put on her good gray dress. She stood at the head of the hall with the Cooperative's bylaws in her hand, and when she called the meeting to order the noise stopped as if cut with a knife.

"This is a special meeting of the full membership of the Carrow Water Cooperative," said Greta. "It has been called under Article 9. There are two motions before it." She read them. The first was the sale: *that the Cooperative sell its rights to the Tern Reservoir and the Carrow canal to Meridian Basin Holdings for fifty-eight million dollars, on the terms published.* It needed a hundred and forty-two votes in favor, being two-thirds of all the households, and absence counted as no. The second was the agreement that Salome had written under the pear tree: *that the Cooperative enter into a fifteen-year lease of its surplus water, and a voluntary fallowing program, on the terms published.* It needed a simple majority of those voting.

"Before the vote," said Greta, "the Reader of the evening will read the sheet."

She stepped aside. There was a stir in the hall, a long, low, rustling murmur like wind in dry grass, and then, from the side door by the kitchen, slowly, in his canvas coat, with his cap in his hand, Odd Bremmer came out.

---

He walked to the lectern as he had walked to everything for sixty years, with the gait of a man crossing plowed ground. He was thin. The coat hung off him. His face was the color of old ivory, and the pale blue eyes were enormous in it, and when he put his hand on the lectern I saw that the hand was shaking, a fine rapid tremor that he did not try to hide. I had set out the sheet for him. I had set it at an angle so that the light fell on the figures. He did not put on glasses. He did not need them. He had been reading figures by lamplight for eighty-one years, and he looked down at the page, and at the hall, and at the page again, and then, in a voice that was not loud, but that carried to the farthest edge of the porch, he said:

"Evening reading. Thursday the fifth of October, six p.m., from the Almanac."

He read it.

> *Conditions: clear, wind NW 14 km/h, humidity 31%, soil moisture at 30 cm: 4%.*
>
> *Reservoir: twenty-six percent of capacity.*
>
> *Next seven days: chance of a wetting rain, twelve millimeters or more, basin average: seven percent. Interval: four percent to twelve.*
>
> *The chance is up from five percent on 28 September. The change is due to a southward dip in the upper-level flow over the coast, which I first saw on the evening of 1 October, and to dew points at Hollin ridge of 12 and 12.4 degrees on the mornings of 3 and 4 October. I have increased my estimate on this basis. The increase is small. It is not a promise.*

He read it in a voice I had not heard from him before. It was not the voice that he used at the grange, the half-bored shout of a man asking for the salt. It was low, and unhurried, and clear, and each number was a thing laid down on the table, like a card. He read *seven percent* as if it were a name. He read *four to twelve* as if it were a pair of dates on a stone. And he read the last line, *It is not a promise,* slowly, with a faint lift on the last word, like a sentence said a thousand times and only now told what it means.

He lowered the paper. For a long moment he did not look up. Then he raised his head, and the pale eyes went round the hall, over two hundred and nine faces, and came at last to rest on mine, on the end of the front row, and he said, quietly:

"This is mine."

There was no sound at all.

"I've been asking a question for fourteen years," said Odd. "I thought I'd tell you what it was for. I'd not known myself. I thought it was a joke. Ruben's joke. *Is that the machine's or yours.* And then I thought it was a dare. And it wasn't either." He looked at the paper. "It was that I wanted somebody to say *mine.* I wanted it said. I wanted it said out loud in the room by a person who could be looked at, because I'd lived long enough to know that a number's a different thing when it's got a face on it. It's not truer. It's not less true. It's *heavier.* You can feel what it weighs."

He drew a breath. It rattled.

"Seven percent," said Odd. "I've been out with the gauge every night for a month. I'd say, if you asked me, that it's not going to rain tonight and not tomorrow. I'd say we'll go on as we are till the middle of the month, and then there's a thing the sky might do. The bees are low. I've listened. I'd not bet my farm on it. I've not been asked to." His mouth moved. "I'm going to vote no. On the sale. I'll say that, since it's what I'd do if I were a Reader. But I'm not telling you what to do. I'd like you to hear what I think, and be able to say afterward that it was one man's, and not the Almanac's, and not the Reader's, and not the bank's." He put the paper down on the lectern. "That's all. That's mine."

He stood for a moment, with his hand on the wood. Then Wren, who had been sitting very straight in the front row with his notebook on his knee, got up, and walked to the lectern, and stood beside him, and put his small hand under the old man's elbow.

"I'll do the second sheet," said Wren, in a clear voice.

"Go on, then," said Odd.

---

They had changed the order. I think it was Hettie's doing. The second sheet, *what would change the number,* was by long custom read after the first, as a kind of coda, and Wren read it from the box that Maud had stood on, in his clean grave schoolboy's voice, with his finger moving under each line. *A southward dip in the upper-level flow over the coast. A dew point at Hollin ridge above twelve degrees for two mornings together.* He paused. "These two have happened. The Almanac says so. They're checked." He looked up. "The third one's the bees. I've been counting them. Grandma's hive three, row two, is the best one for it. In April it was seventy-one in and sixty-two out in thirty seconds. This morning it was forty and thirty-four. That's lower. It's not low. I don't know how low it has to be." He swallowed. "That's mine."

Somebody in the hall made a sound, a sort of cough. I turned my head. It was Dellen, at the back, with his hand over his eyes.

I do not know how long the debate lasted. It was an hour, perhaps, or a little less. I have heard it described since as the best meeting the Cooperative ever held, and I have heard it described as a catastrophe, and I think both are true. Salome Achterberg read her agreement. She read it in the hard clear way that she had said she would, without a word changed, and when she came to *This is mine,* she said it quite simply, in her flat voice, without raising her eyes: "I don't know whether my board will accept this. I'd say the chance is about even. I'd say it's a little less." A murmur went round the hall. She waited. "That's mine."

Ilka spoke. I had not expected her to, and I think she had not either. She stood at the side of the hall, against the wall, with her arms folded, and when Greta asked for any further comment she said, "Yes," in a loud clear voice, and every head turned. She did not come forward. She spoke from where she stood.

"I wrote the technical appendix to the Meridian offer," she said. "Most of you know. It's on the list of consultants. I'm sorry I didn't say it sooner. I'm going to vote for the sale, and I'd like you to know why." She unfolded her arms. "It isn't because I think you can't farm. It's because I've done the arithmetic. In fifteen years the basin's going to run short, whatever you decide tonight. I've checked it six ways. I'd *like* to be wrong. I hope the Almanac's wrong. I hope the whole of the record's wrong." She stopped. "But I've never known a record to be wrong in the direction I'd like. That's mine."

I do not think I breathed. I looked at my daughter across the hall, a tall thin dark woman against a wall, and I thought that I had never seen anything so brave, and so unforgivable, and so entirely my own.

"And I'd like to say one more thing," said Ilka. "It isn't mine. It's my mother's." She looked at me. "A month ago I told her that I'd rather be a dishonest truth-teller than an honest liar. I thought it was clever. I'd like to take it back." She lifted her chin. "I've been reading the second sheet. *What would change the number.* There isn't a dishonest version of that. I don't know what it is. It isn't the truth and it isn't a kindness. It's a *third* thing. And I think if we'd had it fourteen years ago I wouldn't have had to choose."

She sat down on the window ledge. She was crying, and she did not wipe it.

---

The ballots were cast at half past eight.

They went in by households. Each came up to the table in the order of the roll, as Hettie read the names from the green book, and took a pencil, and went behind the screen that Greta had set up in the corner, a folding screen with a pattern of faded roses, and came out, and folded two slips, and dropped them in the slot. It took, as I remember, an hour and ten minutes. It was a thing I had seen done on four occasions in forty years, and it had never before been done in such silence. They came up one by one, men and women, the old and the young, with their coats on and their faces closed, and every one of them, as they put the paper in the box, looked up for a moment at the ceiling, as if the sky might be there.

Odd voted fourth. Wren held his arm. He went behind the screen and was a long time in it, and when he came out he was gray to the lips, and he stood for a moment at the table with his hand on the box.

"I voted no on the first," he said, to nobody. "And I voted yes on the second." He looked down at the walnut. "Ruben made this."

"Yes," said Greta.

"It's a good box."

He let Wren lead him to a chair.

Dellen voted. I watched him go up. He took the pencil, and went behind the screen, and I saw the roses move. He was there for what felt like five minutes. When he came out he was holding the two slips in one big hand, folded together, and he walked to the table and he put them in the slot, together, both at once, with a small decisive push. I could not see what was on them. I do not know to this day.

Ilka voted. She went up quickly, in her field jacket, and did not hesitate, and when she had put in her slips she turned and found me with her eyes across the room, and I saw her give the smallest of nods. It was the nod that one gives to a person on the other bank of a river.

I voted last of the Aldecotts. I had no wish to say what was on my slips. I will say only that it was a thing I had decided on a night in May, in a creamery, in the green light, and that it was mine.

---

They counted in the kitchen. It is a custom as old as the box. Greta and Hettie, and a person drawn by lot from each of the four quarters of the basin, go into the kitchen with the door closed, and the Cooperative waits.

I will not tell you how long it was. I do not know. I only know that the hall, which had been silent, began slowly to hum. It did not talk. It *hummed,* in a low, wordless, communal note, like the sound that a hive makes on the evening before the weather turns. I noticed it first as a vibration in the floor. Then I noticed that I was making it too. Somewhere in the middle of the hall a child fell asleep. A man began, softly, to cough. Wren, beside Odd's chair, had taken out his notebook and was writing something in it, very small.

The kitchen door opened.

Greta came out first. She had the tally in her hand, on a sheet of the board's ruled paper. She walked to the lectern and she stood there for a moment, composed, in her good gray dress, and she looked, I thought, not like a chairwoman but like a teacher about to read out a result, with her whole attention fixed on the child who would take it hardest.

"The ballots cast," she said, "were a hundred and ninety-seven. Fifteen households did not vote."

A long stir.

"On the first motion," said Greta. "The sale to Meridian. In favor: a hundred and twenty-eight. Against: sixty-nine." She paused. "The motion required a hundred and forty-two. It is not carried."

I did not hear what happened next. It was a sound, a great, long, ragged exhalation, the breath of two hundred people who have not known they were holding it; and under it, and around it, a kind of cry, and a thump, and someone saying *oh, oh,* in a high voice. I saw Pieter Vance with his face in his hands. I saw Cormac Teague and Brigid in each other's arms, with the baby squashed between them, protesting. I saw Salome Achterberg at the back, standing alone with her hands folded and her face composed, and then, for a fraction of a second, close her eyes.

"On the second motion," said Greta, over it, in her clear schoolroom voice, and the noise died. "The lease and fallowing agreement. In favor: a hundred and sixty-one. Against: thirty-six." She lowered the paper. "The motion is carried."

I have thought a good deal about the hundred and twenty-eight.

I want to put it down, because it is the thing that Dellen asked me, in the feed store, in August, over a ledger, and I have been trying since to find an honest answer. *Does a true number make people braver, or does it make them run?* Dellen had a hundred and eighteen yeses in August, and thirty-three he could not call. Of those thirty-three, ten had voted for the sale. That is all. Ten. The rest had stayed. The number had been said, plainly, in a hall, in the Reader's own voice, with its interval, and a hundred and twenty-eight households had heard it and gone on voting to sell; and sixty-nine had heard it and voted no; and a hundred and sixty-one had heard it, and read the second sheet, and counted a pencil mark on a string, and had found that there was a third thing to do. It did not make them braver, and it did not make them run. It made them *count.* I cannot put it better than that.

I will not say the hundred and twenty-eight were wrong. I do not think that they were. I think they were people who had listened, and who had done their sums, and who had decided that they would take the money and the house and the rest of their lives. I think that my daughter was one of them. I think that it was an honest vote, and that I would not have given it up for any outcome whatever.

"The bell," said Odd.

He had not stood. He sat in his chair, with Wren's hand under his arm, and in the roaring of the hall I do not know how I heard him, except that I had been listening for it, as one listens for a particular bird.

"What?"

"The bell," said Odd. "It's not been rung. Not for staying." He tried to rise, and Wren steadied him. "It ought to be. Once. For the ones who did."

"For the ones who stayed?" said Greta.

"For anyone who's staying a day longer," said Odd. "For those who'll be counted."

It was Wren who took the rope. He went out through the porch door, through the crowd of coats, in his green sweater, and I followed, and the whole hall poured out after us in a long dark murmuring stream into the clear frost-bright night. The sky was brimming with stars. The dust lay in the road like frozen milk. Wren stood under the old brass bell, a small figure in a green sweater with his two hands on the rope, and he looked back at the people on the steps, at Odd leaning on Dellen's arm, at Hettie, and Greta, and Brigid, and Ilka at the edge of the crowd with her hands in her pockets.

He pulled.

It was not a loud sound. It had been a long time since the bell had been rung by anyone so light. It rose from the porch, a single flat dull brass note, with a long hum at the tail, and went out over the frozen basin, over the roofs and the rye and the dark glimmer of the reservoir, and rolled away down the long slope to the ridge. We stood in the cold and listened to it die.

"That's one," said Wren.

"That's one," said Odd.

"Should I do another?"

"No," said Odd. He had put his cap on. He was smiling. It was a thing I had not seen in a long time, a small, tired, entirely contented smile, like the smile of a man who has set down a load at the end of a field. "That's the right number. Let them count the rest."
