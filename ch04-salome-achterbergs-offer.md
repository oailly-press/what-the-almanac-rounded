# Chapter 4 — Salome Achterberg's Offer

The broker from Meridian Basin Holdings came on the first Thursday in May, and she came in a car, which at Carrow is already a kind of argument.

There are cars in the basin, of course. There are trucks, and the Teague family's ancient green hatchback, and the board chair's electric thing that is always on the point of failing and never does. But the road from the capital comes in over the Hollin ridge in a series of switchbacks that nobody has repaved since my father was a boy, and a car that arrives by that road arrives with a faint air of having made a decision. This one was gray, low, and unmarked, and it stopped in front of the grange at ten minutes past seven, with the sun nearly down behind it, so that when the door opened the woman who got out was for a moment only a shape against the long orange light.

I saw her through the window of the hall, where I was setting out the chairs. I had come early, as I do, but not so early as to be alone, because Hettie was already there and Odd had been there since six, sitting in the front row with his cap in his lap, awake, and meaning to be present for whatever was about to happen. Marit had stayed home. She had, Odd said, a day.

"She's walking like a person who's counted the steps," said Hettie, at my elbow, with a small hard satisfaction.

"Is she?"

"She's looking at the doorframe. She's looking to see if it's been painted." Hettie watched the woman come up the three stone steps. "It hasn't. It needs it. She'll have noticed that, and she'll have noticed that we haven't noticed."

The door opened. The woman came in, and the hall, which had been loud with the sound of people arranging themselves, did what it does when a stranger enters: it did not fall silent, but it lowered. A hundred people, all at once, turned their voices down by about a third, in a way that I think no one decided to do.

She was fifty-four, I knew, from the circular the board had sent around in April. She was of middle height, and her hair, which was gray and cut to the line of her jaw, had been done by a person who did not mean to be looked at. She wore a gray wool coat of an expensive plainness, a coat that would have looked like nothing at all on a younger woman and on her looked like a decision, and under it a pair of boots. I looked at the boots. They were old, a farm boot, resoled more than once, and the leather of the left toe had been scuffed pale by a gate. They were not a costume. I have seen people wear boots as a costume, and the first thing you see is that they are clean.

"Mrs. Aldecott," she said, crossing the floor to me directly, with one hand out. "Salome Achterberg. I'm told that I should speak to you before I speak to them. I'd rather not, if you don't mind. I'd rather you heard it with everyone else."

"Why?"

"Because if I tell you first, it's a briefing. And if I tell you with the room, it's a speech, and a speech has to be something I'm willing to have you hear in front of your neighbors." She withdrew her hand when I did not take it, without any evident offense. "I'm sorry. That was the sort of thing I rehearse in the car."

"It was a good thing to rehearse."

"Thank you." She looked at the lectern. "Is this where you stand?"

"It is."

"I'll use the floor."

---

The board chair called the meeting to order at half past seven. She is a woman named Greta Lindqvist, a retired schoolteacher with a voice like a well-oiled hinge, who had chaired the Cooperative's board for nine years on the understanding that she did not want to and was good at it. She stood at the head of the hall in her cardigan and said that the first item was the Thursday reading, that the second was a presentation by Ms. Achterberg of Meridian Basin Holdings, which the board had agreed to hear without commitment, and that the third, if there was time, was the matter of the headgate on Tern Creek.

I read the evening sheet. It was the usual. A twenty-two percent chance of a wetting rain in the next seven days, I said, which was a little under a quarter; the interval was twelve to thirty-five; the reservoir was at fifty-eight percent and falling; the tier was three. I said it as I always say it. I felt, as I said it, a weight on the back of my neck, as if I had been watched by someone in the hall who had never watched me before, and when I looked up from the paper I saw that it was Ilka, standing against the back wall beside the door to the kitchen with her arms crossed, exactly where Dellen usually stands.

Dellen was not there. I noticed this and did not know what to make of it.

"Is that the machine's number or yours?" said Odd.

"It's the Almanac's," I said.

My daughter's face did not change. It did not need to. I felt the answer leave my mouth as a dry stone leaves the hand, and I knew where it would land.

---

I will report what Salome Achterberg said as accurately as I can, because it matters that I do not make her a villain. I tried to, afterward, for a few days, and I found I could not.

She stood on the bare floor in front of the first row, where she could see the whole hall, and she did not use a screen. She held a single sheet of paper at her side, and for the first several minutes she did not look at it.

"My name is Salome Achterberg," she said. "I work for Meridian Basin Holdings, which buys water rights and the infrastructure that carries them, and sells the water to cities that need it. I want to tell you the first thing, because it's the thing you'll wonder: I am here because I think you are going to run short.

"I don't mean this year. I don't know about this year. Your Almanac knows more about this year than I do, and I'm told you trust it, and from what I've read in the past few weeks I'm inclined to. I mean that over the next ten or fifteen years the basin's inflow is going to be lower than the basin's rights, and when that happens the Cooperative is going to be in a position where the people with the oldest rights and the deepest pockets can survive and the people without them can't. I've seen it. I've seen it twice. In both cases the buyer who came in was the buyer who came in *after*, when the price was down, from bankruptcies, from forced sales, from the courts. I would rather be the buyer who comes in before."

There was a murmur. It was not hostile. It was the sound of a hall that has just been told something it half knew and did not expect to hear said aloud by a stranger.

"I'll give you the offer," said Salome. She did look at the sheet then, briefly, as a person looks at a thing to confirm it is still there. "Meridian will purchase the Cooperative's rights to the Tern Reservoir and the Carrow canal, along with the headgates, the diversion works, and the Almanac's service contract, for fifty-eight million dollars. That's a figure for the whole. The Cooperative's bylaws allocate it by share. On the schedule I've seen, the smallest household would receive a little over sixty thousand and the largest a little under one million. The average is about two hundred and seventy thousand. You keep your land. You keep your house, your barn, your wells, your hives. You keep the right to farm it dry, as some of you do now. What you sell is the right to the water that comes down the creek, and the right to hold it, and the right to say where it goes."

"And where does it go?" someone said, from the middle.

"To the coast. About a hundred and forty kilometers by pipe. There's a pipeline right-of-way that already exists, from when the capital took a share of the old dam, and Meridian has a contract to supply a municipal district that has lost its aquifer." She did not look away. "I'm not going to tell you it's a happy story. It's a city that has run out. They'll pay a fair price to get what you have, and what you have will keep them from a dry tap. That's true, and it's also true that you'll have sold the thing that makes this valley what it is."

A man stood up at the side, a lean red-faced man I knew as Pieter Vance, who farms two hundred acres of barley in the lower terraces and has not, in my memory, said a pleasant word at a meeting. "And if we don't sell?"

"Then nothing happens," said Salome. "The offer stands until the end of October. There's nothing in it that expires sooner. There's nothing in it that punishes you for saying no. If the Cooperative votes no, I'll go home. If you vote no and three years from now you want to talk to me, I'll talk to you. The price, in that case, will be what the price is. I wouldn't pretend it'll be the same."

"Why not?"

"Because if the dry goes the way I think it'll go, the price will be lower." She spoke with a kind of flat evenness that I found myself, to my irritation, respecting. "I'm sorry. I'd rather say that than have you find out."

There was a very long silence. Somewhere in the room a child said something and was shushed. Then Odd Bremmer, from the front, with his cap in his lap, in the voice of a man asking for the salt, said:

"Is that your number or the company's?"

I felt the hall hold its breath. It was a question that had never been asked of anyone but me. It was a question that belonged to the Almanac and its Reader, a family joke, and the old man had taken it out of the family and put it on a stranger as one puts a hat on a post to see how the wind blows.

Salome Achterberg looked at him. She had the grace to take a moment. She took, I would say, three full seconds.

"Mine," she said. "I'll put it in writing. The company's would have a clause."

Someone laughed. It was a small, startled, unwilling laugh, and it spread through the hall as a crack spreads through ice. Odd did not smile. He nodded, once, very slightly, and put his cap back on his knee.

I did not look at Ilka. I did not need to. I could feel her at the back wall with the whole weight of a thing that she had not said.

---

There were questions. I will not give them all. They were the questions you would expect, about the pipeline and the legal title and what would happen to the headgate on Tern Creek, and what would happen if a future Cooperative wanted to buy it back, to which Salome said that it could, at market price, which was an answer that made nobody easier. A woman asked about the grandchildren. Salome said she had two herself and did not know. A man asked what would happen to the Almanac. She said that the service contract was part of the sale, but that Meridian had no plan to replace it, since she had seen the performance record and it was, she said, the best she had read; and that she would be glad to see it kept in Carrow, if the Cooperative wished to keep it, on a license. She said this last in a tone I could not read. I noticed that she looked at me as she said it.

Greta Lindqvist rose at a quarter past nine. She said that the board had agreed, in the matter of the offer, to three procedural steps: that a vote of the full membership would be held on the first Thursday of October, in this hall; that under Article 9 of the bylaws, which tied the water rights to the land, a sale of rights required the assent of two-thirds of *all* households, not of those present, so that a hundred and forty-two votes would be needed, and absence would count as no; and that the board would, in the interim, make available to every household the full documents in the Meridian data room, including a description of the Almanac and its record.

"The third thing," Greta said, and here she looked at me directly over her reading glasses, "is a request. It is not an instruction. For the first time in some years the board would like a formal season outlook from the Reader. In writing, by the first of June. Not the Thursday number; the season. What the Almanac says about the chance of a normal year, with the intervals, and what it says about the reservoir. We will print it, and we will give it to every household, and it will be there when people go into the hall in October."

The room waited. I had, I will say, expected it. It was a thing the board had asked me about, in the way of a reasonable thing asked of a reasonable person, by Greta over a cup of tea on the previous Sunday, and I had said yes. I had said yes, as I have never in my life said anything else to a board. But I had not expected to feel what I felt then, which was not fear. It was something closer to an animal's sense of the floor tilting.

"I'll write it," I said.

"Thank you." Greta nodded. "In your own words, please. The Almanac's numbers in the Almanac's voice, and the Reader's summary in the Reader's. We'd like both."

"Both."

"The sheet and the voice," said Greta, very gently, and I understood with a clear slow chill that she was not being clever. She was not asking me for the two things so that they might be compared. She was asking me for them because it had not occurred to her that they could be different.

---

I found Salome Achterberg at the creamery door at half past ten. I had not meant to look for her. I had walked down the lane alone, having sent Joss to his room and Ilka to put Wren to bed, with the intention of nothing, and she was there, a gray shape on the step in the starlight, smoking a very thin cigarette with the practiced care of a relapse.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I asked someone where the Reading Room was. They said you'd mind."

"I mind."

"I'll go."

"You'll stay," I said, "since you've come." I do not know why I said it. I think it was the cigarette. Ruben smoked, in the years before the Almanac, a thin black cheroot that he cut in half to make last, and I had not smelled one since. "What do you want?"

"To see it." She dropped the cigarette and ground it carefully out on the stone. "I've read about the Almanac for four months. I've read the performance record. Everything the board put in the data room. I wanted to see what it is."

"It's a cabinet."

"I know." She smiled, barely. "I'd still like to."

I let her in. I turned on the lamp, and the green indicator, and the wall of rain cards leapt out of the dark in the same small rows, and she stopped in the doorway exactly as everyone does. But she did not look for the rest. She looked at the cards. She stood for some time, her hands in her coat pockets, reading the brown-inked dates, and the little pencil figures, and the colored tacks.

"Who did these?"

"Odd Bremmer. A hundred and twelve of them. Every morning for sixty years, he writes down what's in his gauge. That's a selection. He gave Ruben the ones for the dry years."

"The ones he thought it would need."

"Yes."

"My father kept a gauge," said Salome. She said it very quietly. "In Vessel Valley. I don't know if you know it. It was a small basin, a lot like this one, a hundred and ninety households. A cooperative. They sold in my twenties, to a company much worse than mine. I was an agronomist then. I'd gone to school on a cooperative scholarship. I was away when the vote was held and I came home to find that it had passed." She breathed out. "I don't say that to move you. I say it because I'd like you to know that I've never been in a room like this one without thinking about the other one."

I looked at her. I had been, I realized, preparing to dislike her for a month, grimly and comfortably, an enemy being so convenient. It is a difficult thing to find the enemy in a pair of farm boots.

"Would you like to ask it something?" I said.

"May I?"

"I'll type. You say."

I woke the terminal. *This is Tamsin,* I typed. *Good evening.* The Almanac answered as it does, with the hour and a note on the soil probe. I told it who was with me, and it said, *Good evening, Ms. Achterberg. You are welcome here,* and Salome, standing beside my chair, read it with a flat, held face, waiting for a bell to ring.

"Ask it," I said.

"May I ask it about the basin?"

"You may ask."

She considered. She was a woman who considered. She said, slowly, and I typed as she said it: *In your judgment, what is the probability that the Carrow basin is agriculturally viable in ten years' time?*

The cursor sat for a moment.

*I cannot answer that,* the Almanac wrote. *It is a different kind of question from the ones I am built for. I forecast weather and water: the rain, the inflow, the reservoir. I can tell you what the records say about the last sixty-one years and what the model gives for the next season, and I can give you a rough picture of the decade, with a wide interval, on the inflow alone. I cannot tell you whether the basin is viable. That depends on prices, on technology, on what people choose to do, on laws, on the cost of water elsewhere. I do not forecast the Cooperative. I would not know how.*

Salome read it. I watched her face. She had expected a sales pitch and been handed a surveyor's note.

*Then give me the decade on inflow alone,* she said, and I typed.

*Over the next ten years, I give a sixty percent chance that mean annual inflow is below the long-run mean, with an eighty percent interval of thirty-eight to eighty-one. I give a twenty-two percent chance that at least three of the ten years are below the level at which the Cooperative's current allocation cannot be met. These are not predictions of climate. They are extrapolations of a record that is not long. I would be cautious about them.*

"Sixty," said Salome, half aloud.

"It's a coin with a bias," I said.

"That's —" She stopped. "I came in thinking I'd be arguing against a number. I was prepared for it to be a good number. I wasn't prepared for it to say *I would be cautious.*"

"It says that a great deal."

"Does it say it to your neighbors?"

I did not answer at once. It was a terrible question, asked in a kind voice by a woman in boots. I felt the whole of the matter lie before me at last like a field after a harrow, every clod turned.

"It says it to me," I said.

"And you tell them?"

"I tell them what I think they can hear."

Salome Achterberg looked at me for a long moment. She did not say what she thought. She said only, with an odd formal gentleness, like a person laying down a card she has been holding a long time and is sorry to play:

"I have to put it in the data room, you know. All of it. What the machine says and what the Reader says. If there's a difference, I'll have to describe it. It's part of the diligence."

"I know."

"I'd rather not find one."

"I know that too."

She picked up her coat from the chair. At the door she stopped, and without turning, said, "My father's gauge is in a drawer in my kitchen. I've never been able to read it. Not because the handwriting's bad. Because I know what it says." Then she went out into the dark, and I heard the car start, a quiet engine, and the long climb up toward the ridge.

I sat for a time in the lamplight. The cabinet hummed. On the screen the cursor waited, patient as an animal, and I thought about the sentence it had written: *I do not forecast the Cooperative. I would not know how.*

It was true. And I had spent fourteen years doing nothing else.
