# Chapter 9 — Commit 884

The drawer is the second one down, under the shelf where the printouts stack, and it has a lock that Ruben fitted himself with a brass key the size of a thumbnail. For fourteen years the key had hung on my ring between the key to the barn and the key to a padlock whose gate had been taken down in a storm. I had opened the drawer nine times. I know, because I counted. I counted the way Odd counts the envelopes, as one counts a thing in the hope that the number will say what the thing is.

We went down the lane that afternoon, the three of us, since Wren had been sent home to his mother with a promise that he would be told what he could be told. It was the hottest hour of the day, and the creamery's tin roof ticked in the sun, and inside it the air stood heavy and brown and smelled of warm dust and electronics and, faintly, of the sausage rolls that Joss had been eating at his desk for eleven weeks. Odd had insisted on coming. He walked slowly, with one hand on my arm, which he has never once done, and on the step he stopped and looked at the door for a time, and said, "I haven't been in since the funeral."

"You can wait outside."

"I'll come in."

He sat in Joss's chair. Joss stood. I knelt in front of the shelf with the key, and found that my hand was trembling so that I could not fit it to the lock, and Odd said from behind me, in his flat kind voice, "Take your time. It's not going anywhere. It's been there fourteen years."

The lock gave with a small dry click.

Inside was very little. There was a paperback book on the care of bees that I had given him one birthday and he had never opened. There was a spool of copper wire. There was a pair of reading glasses with one arm mended in tape. And at the back, laid flat on the wood, where it had lain since the morning after his funeral, when I had first read it and put it back, there was a single sheet of the yellow paper, folded in three, with a name on the outside in pencil: *Tam.*

I took it out. I did not unfold it at once. I knew every word of it; it was not the words I was afraid of. It had a smell. I had forgotten that. It smelled of him, not of his skin, but of the particular room-smell of his hands, pencil and warm metal and the faint sweet cheroot that he cut in half to make last. I sat back on my heels on the creamery floor with the paper in my lap, and for a moment I could not do anything at all.

"I'll read it," I said at last. "I'd like to read it aloud. I've only ever read it to myself. I think I should know what it sounds like."

---

I will set it down here as it is. I have it by heart. It is in his hand, which was square and small and exact, an engineer's hand, with the sevens crossed.

> *Tam —*
>
> *If you are reading this I have not told the board, and I have not told you, and Odd has told you what I told him, or he hasn't, and I am dead. I am writing it at 4 in the morning with the machine humming at my back, and I know that is the sort of sentence a man writes when he is afraid his heart is going to stop, and I am not ashamed of it.*
>
> *This evening I took the courage out of the Almanac. I set it to zero. It is entry 884, and in the ledger where I have written a reason for every change since the first, I have written "ok," because for once it is not an argument. It is a settling. I want you to know that I did not do it because the board was wrong. The board was not wrong. They were afraid and they had cause. I did it because I have been looking at that number for two winters, the little 0.04, and I understood tonight what it is.*
>
> *It is a decision made by seven people on behalf of two hundred, and carried out by a machine that cannot be looked in the eye.*
>
> *That is the whole of it. I do not mind that we soften what we say. I have softened things all my life. I softened your mother's death for you when you were twelve and I was the neighbor's boy; you won't remember. But when a person does it, to a person, in a room, with their own face, they can be asked: why did you say that? Did you mean it? What were you protecting? And they can answer, and be wrong, and be forgiven, or not. A machine that rounds is a rule. A rule is a thing no one is ever responsible for. I do not want to build a basin in which the kindness is nobody's.*
>
> *So I have made the Almanac tell the truth. Always, in every table and every footer, all of it. And whatever mercy there is in Carrow will have to come from someone who can be held to it.*
>
> *You may keep rounding. I know that you do. I have watched you across a kitchen table for thirty years take a bad piece of news and set it down gently, as if it were a sleeping child. You are very good at it. I would not take it from you. But do it as yourself. Say it is yours. When Odd asks his question, answer him. Tell the board at the thaw if I am not there to, and take whatever comes. Or round, and don't tell them, and be the person who knows. That is also a way to live. I only ask that you do not hide behind the glass.*
>
> *Let her do it, or let nobody.*
>
> *I am going to sleep now. For the first time in a long time. I love you more than I have said.*
>
> *R.*

The room was very quiet.

I had stopped reading before the end. I had read the last line with my eyes shut, from memory, the way a person says a prayer in a dark church. The paper shook in my hand with a small dry rattle, like a leaf.

Odd was sitting with his big knotted hands between his knees and his head bowed. Joss, standing at the shelf, had put his hand over his mouth and turned his face toward the wall of rain cards, and I saw his shoulders move.

"*Let her do it,*" said Odd, slowly, "*or let nobody.*"

"Yes."

"He wanted you to know you could."

"He wanted me to say so," I said. "That was the second half. *Do it as yourself.* *Say it is yours.* *Answer him when he asks.* And I read it the morning after the funeral, in this chair, with the green light on the cabinet, and I thought: I can't. I can't stand up in that hall and say *I've been rounding the numbers, and so did he, and the machine's been telling you the truth since the winter.* I'd not been Reader for a day. I'd have been — I thought they'd have taken it from me. Or worse, they'd have put the margin back by resolution and *then* it would have been the machine's again." I stopped. My voice had begun to climb, and I forced it down. "And I thought, he's dead. What can I tell them that won't be the worst thing I could possibly say in the week we buried him?"

"So you said nothing."

"I said it was the Almanac's."

"Every Thursday."

"Every Thursday for fourteen years." I folded the paper along its creases, with an exactness that I could not afford, and held it against my knee. "I let them believe the margin was still there. I let them think the machine was gentle. It was a lie that came to me in the first minute, in the first reading, and I took it because it was warm. And then it was the only thing I had. I have never been able to say *mine.* Because if it was mine then every number I'd rounded in fourteen years was a decision I had made about a person, and I'd have had to look at all of them."

"You are looking at them now," said Joss, in a very low voice. He had turned back from the wall. His face was wet, and he did not trouble to hide it. "That's what the green sheets are. That's what I — I'm sorry. I made you look."

"You did," I said. "Thank God."

---

He had a thing to say, and he took some time to say it. I watched him assemble it, the way I have seen him assemble a column of numbers, a small piece at a time, each laid beside the last. He had taken off his glasses and was polishing them on his shirt.

"I want to say something about the ledger."

"Say it."

"I've read a lot of commit messages." He gave a faint unhappy laugh. "It's, um. It's the thing I do. I read what people write when they think no one will read it. Most of it's rubbish. *Fix bug. Update.* Some of it's lies. There's a kind of person who writes a long, beautiful message to justify a change they know was wrong. I've seen it. They write a paragraph and the paragraph is the apology." He put the glasses back on. "And then there's Ruben. He wrote a reason every time. Eight hundred and eighty-three times. And the one time that he made the most important change in the whole history, he wrote *ok.*"

"Yes."

"It's the only honest commit message I've ever read." He said it with a kind of wonder. "Because the whole point of the change was that there wasn't a reason. There was no argument. There was only the thing. I thought it was a gap. I thought it was an omission." He shook his head. "It's the one place in the ledger where he stopped explaining himself."

I could not answer. I had known the note for fourteen years, and I had never once thought of it in that way, as a man laying down his pen.

"Joss," said Odd, "you'll put it in your report."

"I will."

"All of it. The note."

"I'd like to," said Joss, "if Mrs. Aldecott says so. It's hers."

"It's Ruben's," I said. "And it's the Cooperative's. He wrote it to be found." I had not known I thought so until I said it. "He couldn't tell the board. He was dying. So he put it in a drawer and left me the key. He gave me the choice. He wanted me to choose it as a person. I think he thought I'd choose it by Christmas."

"And it's been fourteen years," said Odd.

"Yes."

"It's not too late."

"I don't know," I said. "I really don't know that."

---

There is a thing that happens when a long secret is told, which I had not expected. It does not feel like relief. It feels like a house after a death, in which every room is suddenly the right size. I sat on the floor of the creamery for some time, with my husband's yellow paper in my lap, and the three of us said nothing, and the afternoon went slowly down the wall.

It was Hettie who broke it. She came in without knocking, as she does, with the particular stiff upright walk that she assumes when she has been told something by a younger person that she had already known. She had her post-office apron on and her reading glasses pushed up into her white hair, and she carried in her hand the green cloth minute book from the post-office safe.

"The boy told me you'd be here," she said. "He came by the counter on his way home and said there was a note of Ruben's in a drawer, and asked if he might tell the Teague girl who comes in for stamps. I told him he wasn't to. And then I thought: well. Someone ought to say it in this room."

"Hettie —" I said.

"We knew," said Hettie.

She said it very simply. She put the green book on the shelf beside the printer, and she turned to face the three of us, and her small fierce upright face was perfectly calm.

"Not all of us. I don't say all. But some. Odd, you know, of course. Marit knew from the way Ruben went on. Greta had her suspicions. There are probably a dozen in the basin, if you counted, who've known there was something odd with the Reader's sums." She looked at me. "I knew from the first month. I posted the sheet. Every Friday, the table, and the footer. Ruben had made me promise to compare them. *Check the footer against the table, Hettie, every week, so someone does.* So I did. And for the first two years the table was off from the footer by four. It was always four. It was in the rules. And then one week in February, I put up the sheet, and the table and the footer were the same."

"And you didn't say."

"I thought: *it's stopped.* I thought: *oh. They've done away with it.* And then I went to the grange on the Thursday and I heard you, Tamsin, say *about a fifth,* and I thought: *oh. She's doing it herself.*" Hettie's mouth tightened, in what I realized was something close to a smile. "I thought, *good.* Because it was the first time in two years that I'd heard it from a person."

"You thought good."

"I thought it was better. A person can be asked." She shrugged, a small stiff movement. "I'd have said so, if anyone asked. Nobody did. And I'd have to say that over fourteen years I never found it to be worse. People got the number they could bear, and they could always read the footer. It's in seven-point type. It's right there. It's the one thing in this basin that was never hidden." She looked at Joss. "He read it. It took fourteen years but a boy came and read it."

"Why didn't you tell her?" said Joss. He said it with great gentleness. "That you'd noticed?"

"Because you don't." Hettie gave him a long look. "You don't tell a woman who's just buried her husband that you've noticed she's carrying his weight. You let her carry it. You make her a custard." She turned to me, and her eyes were bright. "And then it's been fourteen years, and you can't say. That's what happens. It's not that nobody knew, Tamsin. It's that nobody wanted to be the one."

"That's what Greta said," I said, "about the sums. *I know where you looked.*"

"Greta's a clever woman. She'd tell you in her own time. She's waiting for you to tell her, I should think."

"The whole Cooperative's been *waiting* for me?" I said.

"The whole Cooperative has been *going about its business,*" said Hettie, with a certain severity. "Which is a different thing. We're farmers, Tamsin. We know what a rounded number is. We do it every day, and at the grange, and in the store, and to our own children. It isn't that you lied to us. It's that you did what we asked, and did it better than we'd have done, and we let you."

"That isn't an absolution."

"No," said Hettie. "It's a description."

---

I went back up the lane at six in a state that I can only call stunned. The light had gone orange. Dust hung in it in a haze, and the swifts were out, hawking over the orchard in long, dark, hissing curves, and the wind had come round into the north. At the gate I stopped. The kitchen window was lit.

Ilka was at the table with her laptop closed. She had her hands flat on it, like a woman about to give testimony, and Wren was not there. She looked up when I came in. She had, I saw, heard something from someone, or had guessed everything, and was waiting.

"Joss came to the door," she said. "He said I should be here when you got back."

"Did he."

"He said there's something of Papa's you've got." She stopped. "Mama, you're white."

I took the yellow paper out of my pocket. I put it on the table between her two hands. I did not say anything. I could not have. I sat down across from her in the chair that had been her father's, with my hat still on, and I watched my daughter, who has her father's height and my jaw and a good deal of what both of us never said, take the paper by its edge, and unfold it in three, and begin to read.

She read it twice. I could tell. Her eyes went down the page, and back up, and down again, and I watched her mouth move, very slightly, at the place where he had written *I softened your mother's death for you when you were twelve.* I remembered it. He had been fourteen, the neighbor's boy, and he had told me that my mother was only sleeping in the hospital for a day longer than she was. I had never forgiven him and I had never stopped loving him for it. I had not told Ilka that either.

When she reached the end, she laid the paper down on the table very gently, with the handwriting up, and put one hand over her eyes. For a long time she did not speak. Then she took her hand away. Her face was perfectly still, the way a pond is still in the moment before a stone goes in.

"He's right," she said.

"I know."

"I mean about the machine. A rule's a thing nobody's ever responsible for." She wiped her cheek with the back of her wrist, roughly, like a child. "I've been telling myself for a month that the thing that bothered me about the sheets was the *gap.* The three points. The rounding. And it wasn't. It was that I could never tell who to be angry at." She looked up. "You're the only one I could be angry at, Mama. And it was never you. It was always the *Almanac's.*"

"I let you think it."

"You let me think it for eleven years." She was not accusing. Her voice was quite level, and it frightened me. "And Papa wrote you a letter fourteen years ago saying *tell them.* And you didn't tell me."

"No."

"Why not?"

I looked at the paper. I looked at the small square handwriting, with its crossed sevens, and I thought of all the answers I had given, to Joss, to Odd, to the creamery floor, and I found that they had all gone, as water goes into dust.

"Because you'd have gone," I said. "If I'd told you. If I'd said, *the machine has told the truth since the winter your father died and I've been the one who didn't.* You'd have gone to the Authority and you'd have said it in a room, in your plain voice, and the whole thing would have come down, and I'd have been —" My voice broke. "I didn't want to lose you to the truth, Ilka. I'd lost him to it. I wanted you to go on thinking the Almanac was a kind thing."

My daughter did not move. The clock in the next room struck the quarter. Across the table, in the failing light, with her father's note between her hands, she looked at me for a long, long time, and what was in her face I will not try to set down. I have no word for it. It was not forgiveness. It was not its opposite. It was the look of a person who has been handed a very heavy thing, and is deciding, in her own time, whether to take it up.

"I'm going to take a walk," she said, at last. "I'll be back before it's dark."

She took her jacket from the hook, and went out, and shut the door behind her very quietly, so that the latch hardly clicked. Through the window I watched her go down the lane toward the creamery and the ridge, a straight, thin, dark shape in the orange dust. She did not look back. And I sat on in my husband's chair, in the failing light, with the paper that she had left on the table, and I did what I had not done in fourteen years: I said it, aloud, to the empty kitchen.

"It was mine."

It sounded, in the room, like a small thing. I had expected a roar. It was only a sentence, said by an old woman to a clock. But the clock went on ticking, and the dust went on lying on the lane, and nothing at all fell down. I think that was the first thing that surprised me. The second came on the Thursday.
