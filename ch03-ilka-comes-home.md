# Chapter 3 — Ilka Comes Home

I put air in the blue bike on the twenty-fourth, and again on the morning of the twenty-fifth, because the valve is old and the tire is a thing that holds a promise about as long as a politician. It was a boy's bike, twenty-inch, painted the blue of a robin's egg by a man in town who had since gone into the church, and it had belonged first to Ilka, and then to nobody, and then to a set of hens that roosted on it for most of a decade. I wiped the handlebars with a damp rag. I stood it by the gate. It looked, I thought, like a small sincere animal that had been told someone was coming.

The bus came at ten past two. It was a warm afternoon, the first in weeks that had no wind in it, and the dust on the south slope lay flat and gold like the pelt of something sleeping. I walked to the head of the lane to meet it, which I have not done for a person since Ruben's brother came to bury him. Pell brought the bus around the corner slowly, in the way of a woman who understands what is being delivered. The door folded back with a sigh.

The first thing that got off was a bicycle helmet, held by its strap in a child's fist. Then the child, in a green sweater too heavy for the weather, with a notebook under his arm, who stood on the gravel and turned, with great deliberation, through a full circle, as though he were being asked to take a count.

"Hello, Wren," I said.

"Hello, Grandma." He looked at me. He has his father's eyes, which are long and dark and far apart, and he has an expression that I can only describe as *auditing*. "There are fourteen hives."

"There are a hundred and ten."

"No, I mean on the slope. From here. I can see fourteen white boxes." He pointed with the notebook. "It's a sample."

"It is," I said. "It's a very good sample."

Behind him, in the door of the bus, my daughter was handing down a duffel and a long hard case and a canvas bag that clinked, and talking to Pell in a low voice, the way she does, with her head slightly inclined and her whole body arranged to say *I will not be a nuisance.* She is thirty-six. She has her father's height and my jaw, and the cropped dark hair she has worn since she was a graduate student, and a field jacket the color of a wet sandbag. She was thinner than I remembered. She had the careful, even, slightly rigid posture of a person carrying something on her head. When she turned I saw that she had been crying at some point on the long ride up, and had washed her face in a bus toilet, and had done it well.

"Mama."

"You're thin."

"You're tall," said Ilka, which is a joke she has made since she was fourteen and overtook me, and which has never once stopped being the safest thing we can say. She put down the case and came across the gravel, and she held me, quickly and hard, with the duffel strap still on her shoulder, and for a moment I felt the whole thin warm structure of her, a bird's weight, a coiled spring, and I thought: *she has lost weight in the arms.*

"The bike has air," I said into her shoulder.

"Of course it does."

---

You will want to know what a house sounds like when it has had no one in it but one woman for fourteen years and then has three. It sounds, first, like a house trying to remember how it is done. The stairs were louder. The kitchen door, which sticks, stuck loudly, and Wren studied it for a moment and then, with a precision I found alarming, lifted it a quarter inch by the handle and swung it free. "It's the frame," he said. "It's swollen at the top." He wrote this in his notebook.

I had made up the room at the end of the upper hall for Ilka and put a cot in the little one beside it for Wren, the one that was her sewing room, which he accepted without comment and then examined, with a flashlight, for the better part of an hour. There were two bags to unpack, and the long hard case, which Ilka carried upstairs herself, unlatching it on the landing to look at what was inside and closing it again with a small, private, satisfied snap. I did not ask. I knew what it was. It was a field kit. A flow meter, a handful of sensor stakes, a calibrated cylinder, and a folding frame for a weather station, which she had carried to every basin she ever worked in, in the way that a doctor carries a bag. That she had brought it to my house on a leave of absence I noted, and put away in the pantry of my mind, beside the other things.

We ate at six. I had made the stew that I make when I do not know what to say, a thing with barley and the end of the winter's carrots and a ham bone from the freezer, and I had asked Joss, who was boarding above the post office in a room that Hettie keeps for visiting inspectors and occasionally for the lost, to come and share it. This had been a deliberate piece of cowardice. A third person at the table is a wall, and I had wanted a wall.

"Joss Penhallow," said Joss, rising when Ilka came down, with the unfortunate formality of a rehearsed greeting. "From the college. I'm working with the Cooperative on the —"

"On the data room," said Ilka.

"Yes." He blinked. "You know about it?"

"I'm the person they asked to review it." She sat down. She did it with the economy of a person who is not going to waste a movement, and she put both hands flat on the table on either side of her bowl. "I declined. That is, I said I'd decline. Then I said I'd think about it. So I'm either a conflict of interest or a colleague, and I'm not yet sure which. Pass the bread."

Joss passed the bread. He did it with both hands, like an offering.

Wren, who had come down last, and who had been standing in the kitchen door counting the chairs, sat in the one beside Joss and addressed him directly, as one addresses an official who has been sent.

"What's your job?"

"I'm an apprentice."

"At what?"

"Hydrological data. I check whether the numbers a model gives you are the numbers the model has." Joss thought about it. "I look for the places where something has been changed."

"Has anything been changed?"

"I haven't found anything," said Joss, truthfully, "yet."

"What would it look like?"

"It'd look like a number that was different from what it should be."

"How would you know what it should be?"

Joss opened his mouth. He closed it. I saw him think, quickly and sidelong, caught by the one question that was not in the textbook, and I saw Ilka watch him think with an expression of grim enjoyment.

"That," said Joss, "is the whole job."

"Eat your stew, Wren," said Ilka.

"I'm eating it. I'm asking at the same time."

---

It was after the dishes, when Wren had gone out with Joss and the flashlight to look at the hives in the last of the dusk, a sort of cautious state visit, that my daughter and I came to the thing.

We were in the kitchen. I was drying, and she was washing, which is how we have always done it. It is a division of labor she settled on at the age of seven, on the ground that the person who washes can stand with her back to the other one. She did not turn around. She had her sleeves pushed above the elbows, and the window in front of her had gone to a deep blue square, with a reflection of the room laid over it, and in the reflection I could see her face, not looking at me.

"I've been reading the sheets," she said.

"What sheets?"

"The weekly ones. Hettie mails them to me." She said it lightly. She put a bowl in the rack. "She's done it for eleven years. Every Friday, the day it's posted. Folded in thirds, with a stamp. In a plain envelope with just my name. No note. I didn't ask her to. I came home from the first year at the Authority and there was one on the mat, and I thought it was a mistake, and then the next Friday there was another."

I had stopped drying. I held the cloth.

"I didn't know that."

"She said you didn't. She said I wasn't to say." Ilka rinsed a spoon and looked at it. "She said you'd think it was spying."

"It's not spying."

"It's not. It's — I don't know what it is. It's the only thing from home I've ever gotten that wasn't about me." She laughed, quietly, at the window. "I read them on the train. I read the tables. And then I read the footers. I'm a hydrologist, Mama, I always read the footers. And for eleven years I've been reading the real numbers at the bottom of the page, in seven-point type, and then on the telephone on Sunday you tell me what the Almanac said on Thursday."

I did not speak. There is a particular silence in a kitchen that I know from the inside, in which the water goes on running and the clock goes on in the other room, and neither person has yet used the word that would let the other one off.

"You round," said Ilka.

"I don't —"

"Mama. Thirty-eight percent becomes *better than one in three*. Twenty-two becomes *a bit under a quarter*. Eleven becomes *about one in nine,* and you say it with a kind of lift, as if one in nine were — " She turned, finally, with a wet spoon in her hand, and I saw that her eyes were bright and perfectly steady. "I'm not accusing you. I want you to know that. I've been doing the arithmetic for eleven years and I've never once found you outside the interval. You are the most scrupulous liar I have ever known. I mean it as a compliment. I don't know how else to say it."

"I don't think of it as lying."

"I know you don't."

"I give them the interval whenever they ask."

"They never ask." She put the spoon in the rack. "I mean Odd asks, and Odd asks the way you'd ask a dog to sit. The rest of them take the sentence you give them and go home. That's the system, Mama. That's what it's *for*. It's a lovely system. It's a form of love. I've been trying for eleven years to decide whether it's a form of theft."

I will say what I said, because it is the thing I said, and it has not improved with age.

"Your father believed that a person can only hear so much."

"Did he?"

"He — it was after the Bell Year. He saw what happened to people who heard the whole thing at once. He saw thirty-one households —" I stopped. I could hear my own voice, thin and rapid, the voice of a woman who has found a stone in the road and begun to describe its history. "He believed that the Almanac should tell the truth and that the Reader should tell it *so that it could be borne*. That was his phrase. That was the whole arrangement. He put it in the notes."

"What notes?"

"There are —" I did not say what there were. "There are notes."

"I'd like to see them."

"They're his."

"He's dead, Mama."

"Yes," I said, "I'm aware."

It was cruel, and we both heard it. She turned back to the window. For some time neither of us said anything at all, and the water ran until it ran cold and then until it ran so cold that the pipes in the wall began their small percussive complaint, and she turned it off.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That wasn't a thing to say."

"It was true."

"It was true and it was cruel. They're not exclusive." She dried her hands on her trousers, which she has done since she was a child and which I have never been able to stop. "I didn't come to fight with you. I came because they're going to sell it, Mama. The basin. Maybe not this year. But Meridian's offer is a good one, I've read it, and it will have a vote in October, and it'll pass if two-thirds of the households think the water's going to run out. And that is a thing the Almanac will tell them, one way or another, between now and the autumn."

"It will tell them the truth."

"It always does. That isn't the problem." She looked at me in the glass. "The problem is that for twenty-two years it's told them in your voice."

---

I want to explain something about the bees, because what happened in the next hour happened among them, and because Wren set it down afterward in his notebook as a page of counts, which is the clearest account of it that exists.

I keep a hundred and ten hives on the south edge of my land, in four rows facing the morning, on a slope of sorrel and thyme that nobody would have chosen who had not been desperate. They are not a living, but they are close. Their honey is sold, mostly, to the woman in the capital who makes the soap that everyone says is made with other things. They do well in a dry year, better than most farm creatures, because a dry year is a good year for flowers that learned to live with it. And they have, in my experience, a respect for the weather that is more precise than the Almanac's.

They go out in the morning when the sun has been on the front of the hive for twenty minutes. They do not go out on a day when the air is going to turn. They know before I do, by a few hours, and when they know, there is a sound the hive makes, a lowering, a thickening, the sound of a crowd that has decided to stay in and speak quietly.

Wren knew none of this, at the time. He had gone out with Joss and the flashlight into the slope of the hives at dusk, and I followed after twenty minutes with the smoker and my veil, since a hive wants a bit of calm at the end of the day. I found the two of them crouched at the end of the third row. Joss was holding the flashlight with both hands, pointed carefully at the ground. Wren was kneeling in the sorrel, with his notebook on his knee, and his head about eight inches from the entrance of a hive, listening.

"They've got different sounds," he said, without looking up.

"They do."

"This one's a lot louder than that one. I've counted. Over thirty seconds it's seventy-one bees going in and sixty-two coming out, and the one over there is nineteen and twenty. I wrote it down. Why?"

"Because this one is strong," I said, "and that one is weak. It lost its queen in the winter. I'm going to give it a new one in May."

"How do you know it lost her?"

"By the sound. And by looking."

"Can I look?"

I gave him the veil. It was Ilka's, from when she was ten, and it came down over his shoulders like a bell. I lit the smoker, and gave him three small puffs of cool gray smoke across the entrance, the way Ruben taught me, and told him to be still while I lifted the lid. There is a smell when you open a hive in the evening that is the best smell in the world. It is wax and propolis and something like warm bread, and a thread of the sharp sweetness of nectar that is nearly honey, and over the top of it the faint wild alarm of the bees themselves, a sort of bright pepper. He took a breath of it, behind the veil, and I saw his shoulders drop. I have seen it in nine-year-olds before. It is a recognition, I think, of a thing the body knew before the mind was told.

I lifted out a frame and held it up in the last of the light, with the flashlight on it. The bees moved over it like something being poured. The brood was patchy, as I had known it would be, the capped cells in no order, here a solid cluster and there a gap, and nothing in the middle that was not a drone.

"Do you see?" I said. "Where there should be new workers, there are boys. That's what happens when the queen is gone. The workers start laying, and they can only lay boys."

"Can't they fix it?"

"No. Not without help."

Wren considered the frame for some time. Then he said, in the very flat voice of a child going straight past what he was told to what lay under it, "Does the hive know?"

"Does it know what?"

"That it's going to die. If it doesn't get a queen."

I put the frame back. It was the right answer to give, to put the frame back before speaking. I wiped my hands on my apron and looked out over the slope, where the hives stood in their rows like a small white town, and the last of the light was going off the Hollin ridge in a long red seam.

"I think the hive knows something is wrong," I said. "I don't think it knows what. I think it hums differently, and the others in the row hear it, and they all do a little less. I've never been sure that any of it is knowing, in the way you mean. But I've never put a hive in a spot where it could not be heard by the others."

"That's what a number is," Wren said. "Isn't it? It's what you hear."

I looked at him. Behind the veil his face was very small and very exact. Joss, beside him, had gone entirely still, with the flashlight pointing at the earth, and he did not look at me.

"Who said that?" I said.

"Nobody. I thought of it." He looked down at the frame, and then up. "Are you the Almanac, Grandma?"

"No."

"Mama says you are. She said it on the bus. She said, *Grandma isn't the Almanac, but she's what the Almanac sounds like.*"

I did not answer. I replaced the lid, and set the stone on it, and stood a moment with my hands flat on the warm wood.

"I read what it says," I said. "That's all."

"But you say it differently," said Wren, and there was no accusation in it. He said it as he had said that there were fourteen hives on the slope, as a thing counted, a sample, like a boy who had not yet learned that some observations are dangerous. "I'm only asking. I like it. Everyone should have someone who says it differently."

---

Ilka and I did not speak again that night, except to say good night. She went up the stair at nine with Wren's hand in hers, and I heard them in the upper hall for a time, her low voice and his, a question and an answer and a question. Then the house went quiet. I sat on in the kitchen with the lamp. I had the stew pot to scour, and I scoured it with a vigor that it did not require.

At ten I put on my coat and went down the lane to the Reading Room.

I did not intend to ask the Almanac anything. I only wanted to be in the room. I wanted to stand in the small green light of the cabinet, among the hundred and twelve rain cards, and be in the presence of a thing that did not round. The terminal was awake. It always is. I did not touch it, and it did not speak, because it does not speak unless it is spoken to, and I stood for some while in the dark of the creamery with my hands in my coat pockets and the long wind coming down off the slope.

On the shelf was the evening sheet. It lay face up, in the small circle of the lamp. I did not pick it up. But I read it from where I stood, with the gray place in the center of the seeing, and what I read there was the usual thing: the numbers, the interval, the tier, and, at the bottom of the page, in a type so small that I could not make it out without picking it up, the one line that has never, in twenty-two years, been wrong.

*It is not a promise.*

I thought: Ilka read the footers on a train. And I thought, for the first time in my life, that it had never occurred to me to wonder who else did.
