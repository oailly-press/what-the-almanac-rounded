## Verdict summary

This is a manuscript with a genuine voice and a genuine subject: the plain-rural, arithmetic-inflected retrospective narration holds across sixteen chapters, and the best scenes — the hive/gauge exchange in Chapter 10, Dellen folding the packet in Chapter 7, Odd reading the sheet in Chapter 15, the 3:12 a.m. rain in Chapter 16 — are concrete, restrained, and morally legible. The book's central figure, a Reader who rounds a forecast and then must live inside the social consequence of that rounding, is strong enough to carry a novel, and the Almanac is a genuine invention rather than a weather-machine with a personality. What is not yet sound is the book's numerical and chronological spine: the manuscript repeatedly asks the reader to trust exact figures, then contradicts them — population counts, tenure, the courage parameter, the commit ledger, the toll count, the reservoir percentage, the wetting-rain threshold, and the date arithmetic all break under inspection. These are not atmospheric ambiguities; they are load-bearing debts in a book whose theme is precisely that numbers are promises. Several chapters also repeat a completed dramatic move (the absolution beat, the "two systems" thesis, the rounding explanation) and a handful of scenes that should be dramatized are summarized instead. The failures are local and fixable, but they are numerous and they cluster at the climaxes, so the manuscript cannot go forward as-is. **SALVAGEABLE — findings below**

## Suggestions (non-blocking)

1. Build a single numerical bible before line edits: one ledger of dates, ages, household counts, reservoir percentages, toll counts, commit numbers, and interval bounds. The book's theme makes every arithmetic slip a thematic liability, not a copyediting nuisance.
2. Decide the water economy once: either the farms are irrigated and "dryland" is broker euphemism (let Tamsin hear the slip), or they are dryland and the ditch turns must be redefined as something other than irrigation.
3. Resolve Tamsin's tenure explicitly: fourteen years as Reader of Record, or forty years including a co-Readership with Ruben. If the latter, establish the co-Readership in Chapter 1 rather than leaving the forty-year line orphaned.
4. Dramatize the turns currently summarized: the 9 December board vote in Chapter 8, the diligence Q&A in Chapter 4, the drafting-and-deleting of "twelve" in Chapter 6, and the debate in Chapter 15. These are the scenes the book has been promising.
5. Cut or ground the direct-address integrity gestures: "I want you to see what I did" (Ch. 1), "I did not tell him about the last line" (Ch. 2), "I have been accused of making this a tidier confession than it was" (Ch. 7), "You will have to imagine the sound" (Ch. 10). If there is an in-world interlocutor, name them; if not, let the scenes carry the judgment.
6. Give the Almanac a distinct idiolect. In Chapters 4 and 13 it sounds like Tamsin/ the author ("I would not know how"; "A number is a verdict. A condition is a thing one can watch"). Either constrain its diction or supply an in-world reason for its fluency.
7. Reduce the tic load: "I want to," "not X but Y," "very," "small," "thing," "which is," and the "in the way of a woman who..." simile formula. The voice is strong enough without the verbal fingerprints repeating every few pages.
8. Deliver Wren's notebook in Chapter 3 or delete the setup; the same for Hettie's "green cloth minute book" in Chapter 9 and the Moth/Pip drone names in Chapter 5.
9. Price the June 1 threat with one concrete past instance of Tamsin softening a forecast — a named household, a specific decision, a visible consequence. "I tell them what I think they can hear" is the right confession, but it stays abstract.
10. Separate the sheet types cleanly: weekly posted sheet, evening reading, Thursday's sheet, and the "second sheet." The Friday posting / Thursday reading / Sunday call sequence currently asks the reader to reconcile three calendars without help.
11. Bring the institutional stakes forward: plant the sale, the 142-vote threshold, and the hall's inability to carry it in Chapters 1–4 rather than delivering them at the climax.
12. Let Ilka's reaction land on the page in Chapter 9. "What was in her face I will not try to set down" declines the chapter's own climax; one concrete gesture would do more than the withheld summary.
13. Cut the absolution beats from four to two (Odd and Ilka, or Joss and Hettie), and cut one of the three restatements of the "two systems" thesis in Chapter 7 and the five statements of the rounding thesis in Chapter 9.
