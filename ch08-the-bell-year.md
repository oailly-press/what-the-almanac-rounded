# Chapter 8 — The Bell Year

There is a bell on the porch of the grange, and it is not a church bell.

It came off a river steamer that ended its days on a sandbar a hundred miles from here, and it was carried up to Carrow on a hay wagon by the Cooperative's nine founders in the year they dug the canal. It is a heavy, dull, brass-colored thing, green at the lip, with a rope that is replaced every ten years by whoever has the time. In the early days they rang it for water. When the headgate was opened on the first of the season, someone would pull the rope, and the sound would go out over the basin, flat and strong, a note a farmer could hear from the far end of his furrow and know that the ditch had come. After the Almanac, they rang it less. But it was never taken down. It hung there on its bracket, under the porch roof, gathering dust and wasp nests, and children dared one another to touch it.

In the Bell Year, they rang it for something else.

I did not mean to begin with the bell. I had meant to begin, as I had promised Joss, in the right order. But the right order, I have discovered, is not the order of events. It is the order in which a person can bear to hear them, and I did not tell Joss the story that week, as I had intended. Odd told it instead.

---

It happened on the fourteenth of June, a Wednesday, and it happened because of Wren.

Wren had made a friend. This is not a thing that happens easily to a boy who counts, and I had been watching it with the mingled terror and delight of a woman watching a bee land on a child's hand. The friend was Odd. They had met at the fence, over the matter of a rain gauge. Wren had asked what it was; Odd had said it was a gauge; Wren had asked how it worked and what it was for and why the numbers on the side were in a particular ink, and Odd, who had not been asked a question in a year, had stood at the fence for forty minutes answering. The next morning there had been a small, cold, clean-washed jar on my back step with a ruler taped to it, and a note in a very square hand: *FOR THE BOY. IT'S A TEST. TELL ME WHAT IT CATCHES.*

It caught, in the first week, nothing. Wren set it up on the fence-post by the orchard and checked it every morning at six, in his pajamas, with a flashlight and the notebook. Each day he wrote *0.0.* It did not seem to trouble him. It was, he said, a *baseline*. He had picked up the word from Joss.

On the Wednesday morning, Odd came over the fence himself.

I had never known him to do it. He walked, as a rule, only from his house to his barn, and from the barn to the road, and he had been confined, since the spring, to a still smaller geography, the kitchen and the front room and the porch. But he stood that morning at the bottom of my garden in his canvas coat with his cap pushed back, and said, to no one in particular, "I'd like the boy to see the envelopes."

"Which envelopes?" I said.

"The rain."

I put down the trowel. I had been on my knees in the bean row, and I got up with the sound that a woman of sixty-three makes. He was looking at the sky. It was a clear morning, hot already, and the dust on the slope behind him lay in a thin tan sheet. He did not look well. There was a gray in his face that I knew, and a kind of looseness at the corner of the mouth, and he held his left hand a little away from his body as if it belonged to someone else.

"Marit's asleep," he said. "I've been up. I've been thinking about what I'd leave. And I thought, it's no use leaving a drawer of paper to a woman who's asked me to burn it. It'll go to somebody. It should go to somebody who can read it."

"Odd —"

"I'd like the auditor to come," he said. "And the boy. And you. If you'll come. I'd like to talk about the Bell Year, and I'd rather not do it without you."

I saw that his hand was shaking. It was not the shake of age. It was a finer thing, a tremor of the whole arm, as if he were holding a rope that had been pulled taut.

"I'll get my hat," I said.

---

We went up to the Bremmer place at ten, the four of us: Odd in front, in the canvas coat, Wren trotting at his side with a notebook and a pencil, and Joss and I behind, in the heat, along the track that runs between their rye and my hay. It is a half mile. It was the first time I had walked it in a year, and I found that I knew each stone. The rye stood about knee-high, gray-green and thin, with a rattle in it, like paper. In the wet years it will stand to a man's chest. This year it had not tillered, and the rows showed bare earth between the stalks, pale and cracked, the color of an old plate.

Odd's study is a room I have been in perhaps four times in fifty years. It is at the back of the house, off the pantry, a narrow white-walled space with a window that looks north at the barn. There is a desk, a stool, a lamp, and, against the whole of the left wall, a bank of wooden drawers, the shallow kind that a map-maker uses, thirty or forty of them, each with a brass handle and a card in a brass slot. Joss stopped in the doorway, as everyone does. The cards were in Odd's small square print. *'04. '05. '06.* All the way down to the floor, and on the right wall, above the desk, another bank, and another, up to the ceiling, where the last were labeled in a different ink, a blue ballpoint, with the shake coming into the figures. *'63. '64. '65.*

"Sixty years," said Odd. "A bit over. I started when I was twenty. My father had a gauge and he gave it to me the day I got the farm. He said, *it won't tell you anything you can use. It'll tell you what happened.*" He pulled out a drawer at about the height of his waist and set it on the desk. It was lined with a gray felt, and in the felt, in rows, stood small manila envelopes, each as long as a finger, each with a date in pencil on the front. "That's 'thirty-one. Wet. You don't want to read that one. It'll depress you."

"Why?" said Wren.

"Because it was wet and then it wasn't." He took out an envelope and opened it with a thumbnail. Inside was a small folded slip of paper with a single figure, in the same pencil, and a word. "Eleven," read Odd. "*Hail.* That's the ninth of May. The hail took the whole east field. That's what's in the envelope. That's all that's in any of them. The date, the depth, and one word, if there was one."

"Why do you keep the words?"

"So I'd know what the number meant." He put the slip back. "A number's no use by itself. It's a name on a stone."

Wren was looking up at the banks of drawers with an expression of such absolute reverence that I could not look at him for long. He said, in a hushed voice, "Can I count them?"

"There's twenty-one thousand and some," said Odd. "Take your time."

---

He told it sitting on the stool, with his hands on his knees and his cap on the desk, and he told it in a long unhurried flat monotone, the voice in which one says *the creek's up* or *the cow's dry.* I shall put it down as nearly as I can. I shall not try to improve it. There are things in it that I did not know, and things I had known and not let myself know, and I have learned that when a person tells you a thing like that, the only courtesy is to be a clean glass.

"It was the fifth year of the Almanac," Odd said. "We'd got used to it. That's what you've got to understand. For four years it had been right. Not right every day, but right in the way it said it'd be right. It said thirty percent and it rained three times in ten. It said eighty and it rained. We'd none of us ever had that. We'd had the almanac in the paper and the old men's knees. And now we had a thing that told you its reasons and never once said it was sure. You'd have to have lived through it to know what that was. It was like being given a lamp.

"That was the year it didn't rain. It started in the autumn. The first rains were light. The second didn't come. By the first of November the Almanac was giving us twenty percent for a normal year. By the first of December it was sixteen. And on the ninth of December, a Thursday, Ruben stood up at the lectern in the grange, in front of the whole Cooperative, with the hall so full they'd opened the windows, and he said: *the Almanac gives fourteen percent.*" He stopped. "He said it just like that. Fourteen. And he gave the interval. He gave all of it."

"And?" said Joss.

"And nobody said anything. I remember that. I remember looking round the hall, and everyone looking at the floor, and Hettie's husband — you'll not have known him — Hettie's husband saying, very quietly, to the man beside him, *well, that's it, then.* And the man said, *that's it.*" Odd's mouth moved. "It was a good number. It was the right one. It was what the sky had in it. And what a number does, you see, when it's right and it's bad and it's said once to everyone at the same time, is it doesn't tell you what to do. It tells you what everyone else is going to do.

"I'd say about a quarter of us did the arithmetic that night. The rest did the other arithmetic. They did the sum of who was going to leave. *If the Pearsons sell, the price drops. If the price drops, I can't borrow against the land. If I can't borrow, I'm done.* It was only a number. But it was a number that had been said in a room. You couldn't un-say it."

Wren had stopped counting. He was standing very still among the drawers.

"Then the bank," said Odd. "I don't think Ruben thought of the bank. The lender in the capital had been reading the sheet for a year, since the board put it in the post. The weekly sheet. The bank's man had a standing order. He read it every Friday, I suppose, in his office, with his tea. And on the Friday after the ninth, the bank's man looked at fourteen, and picked up the telephone." He breathed. "They didn't call the loans. That's what they'll tell you. They *reviewed the exposure.* They sent out a letter. They said the Cooperative's land was now classed as *impaired,* and that they'd be reconsidering the terms of any renewal. It meant that anyone who needed a loan that winter to plant the spring wasn't going to get it. And everyone needed it."

"So that's what —" said Joss.

"That's what the fourteen did. It wasn't the fear. I wish I could tell you it was only the fear. It was the *paper.* The number went from the hall to the post office to the bank, and the bank did what banks do. The fear was only the weather. The bank was the flood."

He was quiet for a time. The north window had gone gold. A fly was beating itself softly against the pane.

"They rang the bell," said Odd. "That's what it's for, now, if you've never asked. It was Hettie's husband's idea. The first one to sell was a man called Aldous Pearson, in the first week of February. He went on a Saturday. He'd the land sold and the truck loaded, and the old men came and stood on the grange porch, and one of them took the rope and rang it, once. *One.* That's how it's done. One toll for a household. It's a kindness. It means *we know you're going.* And it was only meant for one.

"It rang thirty-one times. In seven weeks. Some Saturdays it rang three. You'd hear it go across the basin, flat and dull, and you'd know without being told that someone's kitchen was being emptied. My Marit stopped going to the window. She said, *I can't watch them load the truck.* We'd sit in the kitchen and we'd count. *That's twenty-two. That's twenty-three.* I don't know why we counted. It was like counting at a funeral."

I realized that I had put my hand over my mouth. I had been in the third row. I had heard the bell. I had stood at my own kitchen window with Ilka beside me, eighteen and home from her first winter at the college, and I had watched the Pearsons' truck go up the road toward the ridge, and I had said to Ruben, who had come in from the creamery with gray in his face and a stack of printouts, *what have we done?*

"And the rain?" said Joss, softly.

"Didn't come," said Odd. "That's the thing. It didn't come till April, and when it came it was no great thing. About half a normal. It was a bad year. It was a bad year. The ones who stayed lost, some of them. But we came through, those of us who did. I did. I put a crop in the ground against everything the sheet said, and I got a third of a harvest, and I lived on it. And the ones who'd gone —" He rubbed his jaw. "Aldous Pearson sold for forty cents on the dollar to a company that's since been bought by the company that's since been bought by Meridian. He's in a flat in the capital. He's eighty-four. He sends a card at Christmas with nothing written on it but his name."

---

"What did Ruben do?" I said.

It was the first thing I had said in a half hour, and I heard how my voice sounded in that small room.

Odd looked at me for a long time. His face in the gold light was very old and very tired, and there was something in it that I had not seen there before: not reproach, but the plain unguarded look of a man who has decided to put down a weight in front of the one person who knows what it is.

"You know what he did."

"I know what he did to the Almanac. I don't know what he did to *himself.*"

"He didn't sleep," said Odd. "That's what he did. He came to the farm on the night of the last toll. March, it was. The thirty-first. He'd had a rope burn. He'd gone to the porch and rung the bell himself, for the Harlans, who were the last. Nobody else would. And he'd stood there afterward with the rope in his hand till it was dark. He came down to my place at midnight. He sat in that kitchen. He said, *Odd, I killed them.* I said, *you did not.* He said, *I said fourteen.* I said, *it was fourteen.* And he said —" Odd stopped. He picked up his cap and put it down again. "He said: *a true number's a thing you can do wrong with.*"

"Yes," I said. "He said that to me."

"He went to the board in the spring. I think you know. He asked for what he called a *resolution of the Reader's discretion.* He wanted the Reader to be free to say a number *so it could be borne.* The board said no. Not no. They said it wasn't enough. They said a thing said in a room was a thing said in a room and the bank would still read the sheet. And a man called Aurelius Fenn, who'd lost his father's farm, stood up and said: *then change the sheet.*"

"The Bell Resolution."

"It was a hard vote. Seven to two. Ruben was one of the two." Odd smiled a thin dry smile, with no pleasure in it. "I was the other. You'll not have known that. I voted against it. I said a man has a right to the true number. I said it was a kind of theft, to decide what a man could carry. And they said, *Odd, you've never been afraid of anything in your life.* And I said *that's the point.* But I'd lost."

The room was silent. Wren had sat down on the floor with his back against the drawer marked *'31.* He had closed the notebook and was holding it in both hands against his chest.

"So the margin went in," said Odd. "Four points, within the interval, on the printed table, and so on the spoken number too, since the Reader read the table. And Ruben made them put the real one in the footer. That was the bargain. That was Article 14." He shook his head slowly. "And it worked. It did. It did what they'd said. People stayed. In the next dry year, and the one after, they planted, and nobody left. We had no more bell. We had, for the first time since the canal, a clear run of years in which nobody rang it. And I thought, God forgive me, *well. Perhaps they were right.*"

"But you didn't think so for long," I said.

"I didn't think so for more than two." He looked at his hands. "And then I did something I should have done at the start. I went to Ruben. It was the February. The winter he died. He'd been working late in the creamery and I'd found him sitting up at the machine with the lamp. He looked ill. I thought it was his heart. He said, *Odd, I took it out.*"

"Took what out?"

"That's what I said. And he said: *the courage.* He said, *I set it to zero.* And I said, *does the board know?* And he said no. He said he'd tell them at the thaw."

I felt the floor go out from under me, in the quiet, slowly, like a lamp being carried from a room.

"He told you," I said.

"He told me." Odd's eyes were on mine. They were the pale washed blue of old denim. "He didn't tell Marit. He didn't tell the board. He told me, because I was the other one, I suppose. And three weeks later he was dead, in the creamery, in his chair, with the lamp on. And nobody ever told the board at the thaw. And nobody told anybody." He opened his hands. "I didn't. And you didn't."

"No."

"You were the Reader. You'd have read the sheet that first week and known the margin was gone. I never knew whether you had. I never asked. I thought, *if she doesn't know she'll find out, and if she knows, she's got her reasons.* And I never asked." His voice dropped. "That's why I put the question. At the grange. Every Thursday. *Is that the machine's number or yours?* It was a way of asking without asking. I thought, one day she'll say *mine.*"

I could not speak. I thought that I would not, ever again.

"And you never did," said Odd, gently. "And I never said. We've been a pair of old fools, the two of us, holding up opposite ends of the same lie, for fourteen years." He rubbed his eyes with his thumb and forefinger, and I realized that he was crying, not as a man cries, with a convulsion of the face, but the way an old well gives water, steadily, from the very deep. "I'm not angry. I want you to know that. I'm — I'd like to say that I never asked for the number. I asked for *you.* I asked for a person who'd stand up on a Thursday and say a thing in a voice I knew. And you gave me that. I'd have had you round it up to ninety. I'd have had you tell me *rain tomorrow* every week of my life. I'd rather have a woman I trust, telling me something that isn't quite so, than a machine that's never wrong."

"Odd," I said. "That's —"

"I know what it is," said Odd. "I'm eighty-one. I know exactly what it is."

---

It was Wren who ended it.

He had gotten up off the floor while we spoke. I did not see him go. But when I looked up from my hands I saw that he had gone to the nearest bank of drawers, the one marked with the years of the Bell, and had opened one, and was standing with a small manila envelope in his hands. He opened it, carefully, with a thumbnail, as he had been shown. He read the slip. His face did not change, but his shoulders drew in.

"It says four," he said. "And then it says, *the bell.*"

"That's right," said Odd.

"What's it mean? Four millimeters and a bell?"

"It's the thirty-first of March. It rained four millimeters. And the Harlans were the last." He held out a hand for it. Wren gave it to him. Odd looked at it for a long moment in the gold light, then slid it gently back into its place. "I wrote the word because I wanted to remember what the number was *for.*"

"I thought you wrote the words so you'd know what the numbers meant."

"That's the same thing, son."

"It isn't," said Wren. He said it without heat. "A number *means* what it measures. A word means what it's *for.* They're different. You can have one without the other." He thought, and added, a little shyly: "I'm going to put the word in, in mine. In the notebook. If that's all right."

Odd looked at the boy for what seemed a long time. Then he laughed, very softly, a low rusty sound, and I saw him put his hand up and pass it over his eyes.

"It's all right," he said. "It's more than all right."

Joss, who had said nothing in an hour, leaned forward from where he sat on the edge of the desk. He had written nothing down. His notebook lay shut on his knee, and his face was composed, and I understood that he had been listening with every faculty he possessed, as if what he heard would not be told twice.

"Mr. Bremmer," he said. "The note. In the ledger. Entry eight hundred and eighty-four. It says *ok.*"

"Does it."

"Do you know what it means?"

"No," said Odd. "But I know who does." He turned his head, slowly, with the stiff dignity of a man much older than his years, and looked at me. "Tell the boy, Tamsin. He's earned it."

I took a long time. There was a bee on the window glass, one of mine, from the south row, a late forager that had wandered up the track. I watched it find the edge of the pane and begin, with a patient, hopeless, methodical industry, to walk it.

"There's a drawer," I said, "in the creamery. Under the second shelf. It has a key. I've had it on my own ring for fourteen years and I've opened it perhaps nine times."

"What's in it?" said Joss.

"His note," I said. "To me."
