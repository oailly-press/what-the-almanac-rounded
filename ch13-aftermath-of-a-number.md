# Chapter 13 — Aftermath of a Number

It was Cormac Teague who rang the bell.

I found him at six the next morning, on the grange steps, in the cold gray light before the sun cleared the ridge, with his back against the porch post and his boots stretched out in front of him and the baby asleep in the sling on his chest. He had gone home, and come back. A brown paper bag stood beside him on the stone, with the top rolled down, and a thermos, and he sat looking at the rope. It hung in the still air from its bracket, a fat gray hemp thing as thick as a wrist, with a worn knot at the end, and I saw that the knot was damp where a hand had held it.

"Mrs. Aldecott," he said, without turning.

"Cormac."

"I didn't mean it to be heard." He said it to the rope. "I mean I did. I meant it to go off. I'd got as far as the porch and I thought, *I'll give it one.* And then I did and it was very loud. I'd not rung it before. You don't, unless it's —" He stopped. "It woke Gwen."

"The baby?"

"She's got a good voice." He put a large hand over the back of the small head. "I'm not selling. I want you to know. I thought I was. I drove home with the windows down and I told Brigid, in the yard, and she said, *you're out of your mind.* Which I think was a no." He gave a small embarrassed laugh. "I've been sitting here since two. Thinking what the bell's for. Whether it's still for what it was for."

"And?"

"I think it's for counting." He looked at me then, a heavy, sandy, sleepless young man with a thermos. "That's what the boy said. Your grandson. At the gauges. He said it's not the number that matters, it's that somebody wrote it down. And I thought, nobody ever counted *us.* When we left, I mean. Not the ones in the Bell Year. My father's cousin was one. They rang it for him, but that was all. Nobody wrote down what he felt." He moved his shoulders. "So I thought I'd ring it once. For anyone who was going to go and wouldn't say."

"For whom?"

"For me, I suppose. To say I'd thought about it." He rubbed his face. "I can't tell if that's stupid."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

I sat down on the step beside him. It was cold, a clean stone cold that comes up through the clothes, and the sky over the ridge had gone the color of the inside of a shell. A wren began in the porch eave. For a while neither of us said anything. I remember the sound of the baby breathing, which was a small wet whistle, and the long slow tick of the dust settling after a night of wind.

"Is it going to rain?" said Cormac.

"Nine percent."

"I know. I mean *you.* Do you think it's going to?"

I had not been asked it before. I had been asked what the Almanac said, ten thousand times. I had never been asked what I thought, except by the Almanac, on a night in May, and I found that the question had a taste, like a coin held in the mouth.

"I think the most likely thing," I said slowly, "is that it doesn't. I think we'll get to the end of September on the reservoir we have and find out then. I think the number's right." I paused. "I think I'd be a fool not to be afraid."

"Yes."

"And I think a person can be afraid and still plant."

"Is that what you'd do?"

"I've forty acres of hay and a hundred and one hives," I said. "I've already planted. It's all I know how to do."

Cormac nodded, slowly, and took the thermos from the bag and poured something black into the cap and offered it to me. It was coffee, so strong it had a skin on it, and I drank it, and it was terrible, and I have never forgotten it.

---

The board met at nine.

I have attended board meetings of the Carrow Water Cooperative for forty years, and I have never seen one like it. Greta's motion had said it would be open to every household, and it was, in the sense that anyone who wanted to come was allowed to; what she had not anticipated, and what none of us had, was that about a hundred and sixty would. The hall was packed to the doors. People stood along the walls and in the aisle and on the stage, and the heat came up off them in a damp, close, sour wave, and Hettie had propped open every window and the kitchen door, so that the sound of the meeting went out over the road in a low continuous roar, like the sound of a creek.

Greta chaired. She had been up all night. She looked, I thought, determined to be useful about a hard thing she had been told, which is the only way Greta knows how to bear anything.

The first item was the tier. There was no argument. It was moved by a man named Fenn and seconded by a woman I did not know, and carried by a show of hands that went up so fast that Greta had to ask for a count. A hundred and forty-one. It is a number I remember, because it was one short of what the sale would need. Tier 4 meant what it had always meant, a cut of forty percent to every household's turns and a ban on irrigating anything but the stock and the kitchen garden, and the people who raised their hands knew it. They raised them slowly, one after another, with their faces set.

The second item was Joss.

He stood at the front with his tablet in both hands. He had put on a tie, a thin blue one, tied in a knot like a man's first, and he spoke in his small precise voice with his eyes fixed on the middle distance, like a boy reciting. He told them what he had found. He told them in order: the parameter, the ledger, the footers, the green sheets. He did not say the word *lie.* He said *a difference between the published figure and the spoken one,* and he said it, I counted, nine times. He said that the Almanac's own record was clean. He said that the Reader had acknowledged the practice on the fifteenth of June and again on the seventeenth of August, and that he wished to record that she had done so before being asked. He said that in his professional judgment the Cooperative had been told, for sixteen years, in the footer of every weekly sheet, the true figure. And then he said something I had not expected.

"I'd like to put on the record that I think this is the best-governed system of its kind I have ever audited," said Joss. "I know that's a strange thing to say in this room, today. But I've looked at fourteen. I've looked at systems that were never wrong in public and were wrong every day in private. This one was the other way round. It's been wrong, a little, in public, in a way that was written down in the small print, every Friday, for sixteen years, and it has been right in private, in the Almanac, every single day." He swallowed. "I don't say that to excuse anyone. I say it because it's what I found."

Pieter Vance stood up.

"Is that your professional judgment," he said, "or are you being kind?"

There was a silence. Joss turned the tablet over in his hands.

"I'm trying very hard not to be," he said.

Someone laughed. It was a startled, short, relieved bark, from the back of the hall, and it spread, and went round, and for a moment the whole packed room was laughing, in the way that people laugh in the minute after a thing has been said that has cleared the air without making it any easier to breathe. Pieter did not smile. But he sat down.

It was the third item that I had been dreading.

---

Greta read it out in her teacher's voice, in a single level breath. It had come in the night. It was a petition, signed by forty-one households, and it had been slid under the grange door in a plain envelope, and it said that the signatories requested that the board consider, at its earliest convenience, *the future of the office of Reader.*

"I'll read the operative paragraph," said Greta. "*We do not question the integrity of the present Reader, who has served the Cooperative for fourteen years. We note, however, that on her own account she has for that period altered the figures given to the membership, in what she describes as good faith. We request that the board consider whether the Cooperative is better served by (a) the abolition of the office, the Almanac's printed sheet to be posted and read in the hall by the chair; or (b) the retention of the office on terms that bind the Reader to read the sheet without alteration.*"

She laid it down.

"There's a second petition," said Greta. "It came this morning. It's from sixty-three households. It asks that the office be retained, without terms, and that the Reader be thanked."

A small sound went through the hall. It was neither a laugh nor a groan.

"I'd like to hear from the floor," said Greta. "I'd like to say, before I do, that I'm not going to rule on the merits. I'm going to listen. The Reader has asked to speak last."

I had. I sat on the end of the front row, with my hands in my lap, and I listened.

It went on for an hour and forty minutes. I will not give it all. It was the kind of debate in which every person says what he has said before, but for the first time with an air of meaning it. A man said that the whole thing was a scandal, and that a Reader who could not be trusted to read was no Reader at all. A woman said that she had been receiving *about a third* and *a little better than one in four* for fourteen years and had made, on that strength, nine good decisions and one bad one, and that she would not have it called a fraud. A young man I did not know said that the Almanac was the only honest thing in the basin and that it should be given a voice. Someone said that a machine that could not be argued with was the last thing they wanted at a grange. Someone said that was the whole point.

Hettie stood. She stood with the green book against her chest, small and upright, and for a moment said nothing at all.

"I keep the minutes," she said. "I have for fifteen years. And I'd like to say what they show." She opened the book. "On the seventeenth of August, for the first time since the Resolution, the Reader read the figure that the Almanac gave. *Nine percent.* And in the minutes, in the column where I write what the Reader said, it matches the column where I write what the Almanac printed. It's the first time in my book that the two are the same." She looked up. "That's not an argument. It's a fact about my handwriting. But I thought you ought to know I'd noticed."

She sat down.

Dellen did not speak. He stood at the back by the kitchen door, where he stands, with his arms crossed. But at the end of the debate, when Greta asked whether there was any further business, he unfolded his arms, took the ledger from the pocket of his coat, and walked down the center aisle with it in his hand. The hall watched him. He went to the side table, where Greta sat with her pen, and put the book in front of her.

"I'm resigning as treasurer," he said.

"Dellen —"

"I'm not resigning from the Cooperative. I'm resigning the office." He had a plain flat steady voice, a feed-store voice, and it carried. "I've been keeping a count. Of the vote. I've kept it since May. It's the Cooperative's business, I'd say, and it was a matter of an honest man knowing where he stood, but I've been looking at it for three months and I've decided it's not mine to hold. A treasurer shouldn't have it. A treasurer's a man who knows who owes what." He tapped the cover. "I'll vote in October like everyone. I'll say how. But I'd like a clean book to do it from."

"What's in it?" said Greta, very quietly.

"A hundred and eighteen. And sixty-one. And thirty-three." He looked round at the hall, at the hundred and sixty faces. "As of last Tuesday. It's changed since. It's changed since last night, I should think. I'd not want to guess which way."

There was a long, a very long silence. I saw people look at one another, and look away. It was a thing no one had said in front of the whole Cooperative, since the founding: a number for how many of us were afraid.

"Give it to Hettie," said Greta, after a time. "She's the only one of us who can be trusted not to read it."

"I'll read it," said Hettie, with dignity. "But I'll not say."

---

I spoke last.

I will not set down all of it. I said that I understood the petitions. I said I would abide by whatever the board decided, and that if the board decided to abolish the office I would hand the key to the creamery to the chair that afternoon and go home to my bees. I said that I had been wrong, and that I did not think I had been wrong in the way the first petition meant, nor in the way the second did; that I thought I had been wrong in a way that neither of them had a word for, which was that I had been *the only one.*

"I have been the only voice," I said. "For fourteen years. That's the fault. I don't say that the rounding was right. I say that it was the sort of thing that should never have been done by one person, in a room, on a Thursday, with nobody to say no. My husband knew that. It's why he took the margin out. He wanted it to be *someone's.* He didn't want it to be mine alone. He wanted it to be *ours.*" I had not known I was going to say it. "I would like the board to consider, before it decides, whether the question is who reads the sheet. Or whether the question is how many."

It is not a thing I had planned. I sat down. I did not know what I had said.

It was Greta who understood it first. I saw her put the end of her pen against her teeth, and look at me, over the top of her glasses, in the way that she looks at a child who has said something better than she knew.

"The board," she said slowly, "will take that under advisement."

---

I went down to the creamery at noon, because it was the only place I could think of that was not full of people. The door was open. I saw before I reached the step that someone was in the chair.

It was Brigid Teague.

She is a thin, quick, dark young woman, a good deal younger than her husband, with a way of holding her body as if it were a wire stretched between two posts. She had her coat on, in the heat. She was sitting very straight in the second chair with her hands folded on the shelf, and she did not stand when I came in.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I let myself in. Joss said it'd be all right. He said you'd be along."

"It's all right."

"I want to ask you for something." She did not look at me. She looked at the black cabinet. "I know it's not right. I know what the Reader's for now. Cormac told me. He said you'd say *no,* and he said it'd be the best thing about you." She drew in a breath. "But I'd like to ask."

"Ask."

"Will you ask it to say something kind?"

I stood in the door. Behind me the yard was white with sun, and the dust lifted a little and fell.

"Not a number," said Brigid. "I don't mean a number. I mean *something.* Anything. The baby's — Gwen's six months old, Mrs. Aldecott. I've got forty acres and a mortgage and a husband who sits up till two on the grange steps ringing bells. I heard you read it. *Nine percent.* I thought, I'll go home and I'll be sick. And I was." Her mouth twisted. "And I thought, *it's a machine. It's got to have something it can say.* A thing that would make me able to get up in the morning. It doesn't have to be a lie. It can be a thing that's *true.* There must be something that's true and also not that."

She looked at me then. Her eyes were black and very bright. I had never seen anything so naked.

I went to the terminal. I woke it. I typed, with a hand that was not entirely steady: *This is Tamsin. Good afternoon. Brigid Teague is here with me. She has a question.*

*Good afternoon, Tamsin. Good afternoon, Mrs. Teague.*

I read Brigid's words aloud as I typed them, slowly, as she gave them to me, and I typed them exactly, without changing a syllable. When I came to the end I sat back, and the screen was still, and I thought, for the first time in my life, with a clean cold terror, that I did not know what it would say.

The cursor waited. It waited for some time.

*I cannot say that it will rain,* the Almanac wrote. *I will not say a number that is not mine. I am sorry. I understand what is being asked, and I understand that it is not a small thing.*

*But there is something else I can do, and I would like to offer it, if it is wanted.*

*I have been telling the Cooperative what the chance of rain is. I have not been telling it what would change the chance. This is a different kind of information, and it is, I think, of more use to a person who is afraid. A number is a verdict. A condition is a thing one can watch.*

*The chance of a normal year is nine percent because the large-scale circulation that brings the autumn rains to this basin is, at present, weak and in the wrong place. If it were to shift, the chance would rise. I can tell you what the shift would look like. I can tell you the three signs I would see first.*

*The first is a southward dip in the upper-level flow over the coast, which I would see on the radio feed from the capital. The second is a rise in the dew point at the Hollin ridge station, above twelve degrees, for two mornings together. The third is the sound the Reader has described to me from the hives. I do not understand it. But I have noticed that in the eight years the Reader's husband kept the ledger, he wrote on eleven evenings that the bees were low, and on nine of them it rained the next day. I cannot confirm it. I have no measurement. I would give it very little weight. But I give it some.*

I stopped reading. I could not go on. Brigid, beside me, had put her hand over her mouth.

*I can print these as a second sheet each week,* the Almanac continued. *"What would change the number." I would not say that they will happen. I would say that if they did, I would change what I gave, and by how much, and that the Cooperative would see it before I did, in the sky and the gauges, and could tell me. That is the only form of hope I can honestly offer. It is a hope that is made of watching. I think it is also the only form that a person can do something with.*

It was very quiet. In the yard a wasp had found the window and was walking up and down the glass.

"It's *true,*" said Brigid. She said it in a whisper. "It's all true."

"Yes."

"And it's not that." She took her hand from her mouth. She was crying, quite silently, with her chin up, the tears running straight down her face without her seeming to notice them. "It's a thing to do. It's a thing to look at, in the morning, instead of the number. Mrs. Aldecott. It's *work.*"

"Yes," I said. "I think that's what it is."

"I'll watch the dew point." She laughed, wetly. "I don't know what a dew point is. I'll learn."

"I'll show you."

"Will you?"

"I'll ask the boy. He'll tell you. He's — Joss. He'll have a chart." I found that I was smiling, and that my own face was wet. "It's a very good chart. It's got colors."

We sat there for some time, the two of us, in the heat of the creamery, with the cabinet humming and the little green light and the wall of old rain, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing in the world that wanted saying.

---

The second sheet went up the next Friday. Hettie pinned it beside the weekly one, on the corkboard, in the place where the footer had been for sixteen years, and under it she taped a pencil on a string. The title was in the Almanac's own plain type. *WHAT WOULD CHANGE THE NUMBER.* Beneath, in three short paragraphs, the signs: the dip, the dew point, the bees. And at the bottom, in the smallest type that the printer would allow, the line that had stood at the bottom of every page for twenty-two years.

I will not say that the people read it. They did not, at first. But on the Monday there was a pencil mark on it. And on the Tuesday a second. And on the Wednesday I came into the post office for stamps and found a small grave child of eight, standing on an upturned crate, laboriously writing on the margin, in block capitals, with her tongue between her teeth: *DEW 11. 11. 12.*

I did not ask what it meant. I stood at the counter with my coins and watched her put the pencil back on its string. Then she got down off the crate, looked up at me with extraordinary solemnity, and said, "Twelve's the one."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

"I'm *waiting* for it," she said. And went out, banging the door.

On the second of September, the reservoir fell below the intake. It was a Saturday, a clear, hard, still morning. The Almanac had said that it would, and it did, and at eleven o'clock the pump on Tern Creek gave a long metallic sigh and drew air, and Dellen's lorry went out to the Bremmer place with four thousand liters, and nobody rang the bell.
