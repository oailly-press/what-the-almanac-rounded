# Chapter 7 — What Joss Found

The Almanac can replay any day it has lived through.

I had known this for twenty-two years and had never once thought about what it meant. When Ruben built it he fed it sixty years of the basin's weather, one day at a time, and he taught it to behave, in the training, as though each day were the present: to take only the observations that had been in hand on that morning, and to give a reading, and then to be told what had happened next, and to learn. It did this some twenty-two thousand times. It has kept the habit. If you say to the Almanac, *give me the evening reading for the ninth of March, six years ago, using only what you knew at six o'clock that day,* it will withdraw into itself for a moment and return with a sheet, the same sheet it printed then, to the digit, with the interval in its margin and the footer in seven-point type. It does not remember. It reconstructs. It is the most honest form of memory I know of.

Joss discovered this in the second week of June. He discovered it the way he discovered everything: slowly, with a pencil, and a small embarrassed delight.

I had given him, as Marit asked, a day off. He had spent it in the Reading Room.

---

The first I knew of his method was a graph. He did not show it to me. I came into the creamery on the Thursday morning, the eighth, at seven, to find the shelf laid with sheets of the pale green graph paper that the college issues to its apprentices in packs of five hundred, each sheet covered in a fine and tremulous hand, and Joss himself asleep in the second chair with his head on his arm and a pencil in his fist. He had not shaved. The thin jacket was over the back of the chair. In the window the light had begun to be the clean pink of an early June morning, and across his sleeping cheek the wall of Odd's rain cards had put a faint dappled pattern of colored tacks.

I did not wake him. I looked at the paper.

I am not a statistician. I am a keeper of bees and a reader of other people's sentences. But I have spent twenty-two years in a room with a machine that speaks in intervals, and I have learned, as one learns the shapes of the stars without knowing their names, to read the outline of an argument in a column of figures. What lay on the shelf was a pair of columns. In the left one, in a firm slanting hand, were a long run of dates, each followed by a number. In the right, in a slightly less firm hand, were more dates, with other numbers. Between them, down the whole length of the sheet, ran a faint pencil line, and at the foot of the page a third column had been added, with a bracket and a figure and, beneath it, underscored twice, a single word.

*Human.*

I put the sheet down. My hand was quite steady, which interested me, in a distant way, as one is interested in a thing happening to someone else.

"It's a Thursday series," said a voice behind me.

Joss had woken. He was sitting straight, blinking, with a crease in his cheek from the sleeve, and he had the look of someone caught out who has decided in the first second to confess.

"I wasn't going to leave it there," he said. "I mean, I fell asleep. I was going to — I wanted to show it to you before I showed it to anyone. If I showed it to anyone. I'm sorry. I'm — can I get you some tea? I've got a kettle."

"I know about the kettle."

"Right." He looked at the paper in my hand, and then at me, and then, with a visible gathering of will, he said: "Would you like me to explain it? You don't have to. I can put it away."

"Explain it."

---

He explained it for forty minutes, and I shall give it in the order that he gave it, since it is the order in which I understood it, and since I have since come to think that the order of an explanation is a kind of moral act.

He had started, he said, with the footer. "It's the strangest thing in the whole system, you see. In a governance review, you look for the place where a thing is disclosed. And there it was: a line in seven-point type at the bottom of a weekly sheet, giving the unrounded values and the intervals. Article 14. It's an extremely careful disclosure. It's *too* careful. Nobody builds a disclosure like that unless they think someone will need to rely on it. So I asked myself what it was for. What was it protecting?"

"Against what?"

"Against a difference." He drew a short line on the green paper. "If the sheet and the footer were the same, there'd be no reason to print the footer. So I thought: there's a difference somewhere. Between what the Almanac computes and what it says. And I wanted to find it."

"And you asked it."

"I asked it, and it said it had adjustments. Calibration, blending, presentation. It listed them. And there was a parameter in the list called courage, and it was set to zero." He did not look at me. "I thought that was a very odd name for a parameter. So I asked the Almanac what it was, and it said it was a historical setting, and that it could tell me about it. And it did. It told me, in a line, that *courage* had once been a fraction, four hundredths, and that it had added that fraction, as four points, to the central estimate it printed in the table, within the bounds of the interval, and that it was now zero."

I said nothing.

"So I looked at the history. Ruben kept the configuration in a ledger, the way a — it's a version log. Every change, who made it, when. There are eleven hundred and sixty-two entries. Eight hundred and eighty-four are Ruben's. The rest are the Almanac's own, the seasonal calibrations it writes into the same book in a plainer hand, each with its formula for a reason. I read them all." He rubbed his eye with the heel of his hand. "It's one of the most beautiful records I've ever seen, Mrs. Aldecott. He wrote a line with every change. Not just what, but why. *Raised blend weight on the analog set; it kept undershooting the dry years. Tightened rounding to whole points; the decimals were a lie.* He wrote like a man who expected to be audited."

"He did."

"Yes." Joss smiled, briefly. "And in the ledger there's a gap. Not a gap, a — a change. In the sixth year of the Almanac, after the Bell Year, there's an entry. *Added presentation offset 'courage', default 0.04, per resolution of the board.* And there's a reference to the minutes. I found them. They were in the files. The board voted, that winter, to adopt what it called the Bell Resolution, that the Almanac should apply a margin to the estimates it published, to be 'a measure of encouragement in lean years,' within the limits of its stated interval." He turned the page. "It carried by a vote of seven to two. Ruben dissented. He lost."

"I remember."

"So the Almanac added the margin, and it printed, in the footer, the real value. That was Ruben's price. He'd accepted the margin if the truth was always on the page, at the bottom, where anyone could read it." He drew another short line. "That's Article 14. It was written the same week."

I was aware of the room. The hum of the cabinet, the little green light, the slow turning of the first sun across the wall. I was aware of Odd's rain cards, each of them bright, each with its small bright tack. And I was aware that I was standing in a room with a person who had been given, by a machine that cannot lie, every piece of a story that I had spent fourteen years hoping would stay in the dark.

"And then?" I said.

"And then it changes," said Joss. "Entry eight hundred and eighty-four. Fourteen years ago, in the winter. The setting goes from point-zero-four to zero. And the line in the ledger, where Ruben always wrote *why* —" He stopped. "It says *ok.*"

"Ok."

"That's all. The only entry in eleven hundred and sixty-two without a reason." His voice had gone very quiet. "I don't understand it. I've been over and over it. I've tried to think what *ok* might mean. It isn't — he wasn't a man who wrote *ok.*"

"No," I said. "He wasn't."

---

It was then that he showed me the green sheets, and I understood what I had been looking at.

He had done a thing that I would not have thought of, and that I think no one before him had thought of, because no one before him had been twenty-four, and patient, and in possession of a machine that could replay the past. He had taken every Thursday evening reading of the past fourteen years, seven hundred and some, and had asked the Almanac to reconstruct it, and compared the reconstruction with what had been printed on the sheet. They had matched. To the digit. Every one. Which meant that since entry eight hundred and eighty-four the Almanac had told the Cooperative the truth, in print, in the table, in the footer, with no margin at all.

And then he had taken the minutes.

The Thursday minutes are kept by the board's secretary, who for fifteen years has been Hettie Marsh, in a ledger with a green cloth cover that she keeps in the post office safe. They are exact. They record the date, the attendance, the headgate schedule, and the figure given by the Reader for the week's chance of a wetting rain, each in Hettie's small upright hand, as *Reader gave 31%, interval 18–46.* Joss had gone through the post office safe on Tuesday, with Hettie's permission, and had copied every one of the numbers that the Reader had given.

He had laid them side by side.

"The left column is what the Almanac printed," he said, "and the right is what was said at the grange. The difference is the third column. I subtracted."

I looked at the third column. It ran down the page in his careful fingers, a long dotted procession of small numbers: *+2, +3, +3, +4, +2, +3, +1, +4, +3.* Here and there a *0*. Once, a *+7*, in the winter of a year I remembered. Never a minus.

"Mean three-point-one," said Joss. "Median three. Standard deviation about one and a half. It's — I've never seen anything so *regular*. I ran it three ways. You know what it looks like, don't you? It looks like a person who always rounds up toward the top of the interval and never past it. A person who has a rule." He swallowed. "I've been trying for two days to think of any other explanation. A transcription error. A habit in the secretary's hand. I couldn't."

"There isn't one."

"I know. I'm — Mrs. Aldecott, I want to say clearly that I'm not —" He stopped, and began again. "I don't think it's a bad thing. I mean I don't know what I think. I think it's a *thing*. It's a feature of the system. It's the most important feature of the system, and it isn't in the documentation, and it isn't in the Almanac. It's in you."

"Yes."

"I think there are two systems." He said this with great care, like a man laying a plank across a stream. "The machine, which is honest and doesn't round. And the Reader, which does. And the Cooperative thinks they're one."

"Yes."

"And I think —" He looked down at the paper. "I think they've been told they're one. By you. Every Thursday. *It's the Almanac's.* That's what Mr. Bremmer asks. *Is it the machine's or yours.* And you say —"

"I say it's the Almanac's."

"But it isn't."

"No."

It was very quiet. There was a wren somewhere in the eaves of the creamery, singing in the dry loud way of its kind, and it went on, and on, and the sun came across the cards and moved up the wall. I had thought, for fourteen years, that if the thing were ever said aloud I would feel it as a falling. It was not like that. It was like setting down a bucket that I had been carrying so long I had stopped being aware of the weight, and discovering, in the instant it left my hand, how much of my own arm it had become.

---

I told him the rest of it a little at a time, standing at the window, with my back to him. I did not tell him all. I want to be exact about that, because I have been accused of making this a tidier confession than it was. I told him that I had rounded since the first Thursday. I told him that Ruben had taught me nothing, that there had been no handing over, no ceremony. I told him that on the morning after the funeral, a Wednesday, I had come into the creamery to find a new sheet on the shelf, and I had read it, and that when the board asked me on the Thursday to stand at the lectern I had read it, and that the number was nineteen, and that I had said, *about a fifth*, without once having decided to.

"I didn't know I was doing it," I said, "until I'd done it the third or fourth time."

"But after that you knew."

"After that I knew."

"And you kept doing it."

"I kept doing it." I looked at the ridge. "I thought I was keeping something going. I thought it was what he would have done. I thought the machine did it and I was only — continuing."

"But the machine hadn't done it since entry eight hundred and eighty-four."

"No."

"And you knew that."

I did not answer. I put my hand on the window frame and I felt the dry old paint under my fingers, flaking in the heat. A very long time passed, perhaps a quarter of a minute, perhaps more. I have found, in the years since, that one can say many things in such a pause, and that the only thing one cannot say in it is *no*.

"I knew," I said.

He did not speak. I was grateful. I think if he had spoken I would have turned and seen his face, and I do not know what I would have seen there, and I was not ready. I heard instead the soft sound of a pencil being put down on a sheet of paper, and a chair creaking, and the quick, tired, adult sound of a young man breathing in and out through his nose.

"Can I tell you why I do this?" said Joss, after a time.

"Yes."

"It isn't a — it's not a good story. But I think you should have it." He had stood and come to the other end of the window, and was looking out at the same ridge, with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders drawn up around his ears. "I grew up in the delta. On the Penhallow side, on the flats. There's a river there, a big slow one that goes out into the salt, and every spring it comes up. Not much. A few feet. You know it's coming. There's a gauge at the bridge, and the council puts out a notice. *One-in-a-hundred-year flood,* it said, the year I was nine. A one-in-a-hundred. We lived in a shop. My father sold hardware. He'd bought the shop outright, with a loan, in the autumn, because the notice that autumn said the river was safe. *Once-a-century.* It's what they say."

"It came."

"It came three times in ten years." He said it very simply. "Not the same size. But each of them was what they'd call a hundred-year. Because the number was wrong, you see. It had been right for a hundred years and then it wasn't. And what I remember is not the water. It's my father, standing in the shop in his boots with the water to his shins, saying, *they said one in a hundred. They said it.* And my mother said, *they didn't say it would not happen, Jonas. They said how often.*" He rubbed his face. "She's a pharmacist. She's very exact."

"Is that why you —"

"I think it's why I check the footers." His mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "A number's a promise to whoever hears it. Even when it says it isn't. And nobody ever audits the people who *say* them. You audit the model. The model's usually fine. It's the sentence that goes wrong, between the model and the ear."

I turned then, and looked at him. He was a thin, ill-shaven boy in a shirt buttoned to his throat, with a cowlick, standing at the window of a creamery in a country he did not know, and I thought that I had never in my life been so thoroughly seen, and that it was the kindest thing that had been done to me in fourteen years.

"What are you going to do?" I said.

"I have to put it in the report," said Joss. "I'm sorry. I have to. The data room closes on the thirtieth. It's a material fact. A prospective acquirer is relying on the Almanac's published figures as the Cooperative's view of its own risk, and the Cooperative's view isn't the Almanac's figures. It's a — it's a discrepancy in the governance." He said it as if reciting a thing he had tried on in the mirror. "I'd like to tell you before it's filed. I'd like to give you time. And I'd like to describe it fairly, if you'll let me. I won't say it's deception. I don't know that it is."

"Say what you found."

"I'll say what I found."

"That's all I'd ask."

"Mrs. Aldecott." He said it at the very last, looking not at me but at the ridge. "The ledger. Entry eight hundred and eighty-four. It says *ok.* Do you know what that means?"

"Yes."

"Will you tell me?"

"Not today."

"All right," said Joss.

"Soon," I said. "I'd like to tell you in the right order. I think he'd want it told in the right order."

"All right," Joss said again, and I heard in the two words a quality that I have come, since, to prize above almost any other: the quality of a person who will wait to be told.

---

The packets went out on the Friday.

I had not meant to go to the post office, and I went. It is the only building in the basin where one can stand for an hour without anyone asking what one is waiting for. Hettie's counter is a long scarred slab of oak behind which she sits on a high stool with a cat on the sorting table and a canary in a cage on the sill, and on this particular Friday, from nine o'clock onward, a line formed, the line that forms every week, for the weekly sheet and the post and the small oddments of news. The board's June packet lay in a tall stack at her elbow: Greta's cover note, the Reader's summary on top, the Almanac's season outlook behind, the whole of it clipped together at the corner with a bulldog clip.

I stood at the end of the counter, by the scales, and watched them take it.

I do not know what I had expected. I suppose I had imagined that people would read it at once, at the counter, in a circle, that there would be a murmur, a turning of heads. There was nothing of the kind. They took the packet from Hettie's hand as they take every paper, with a nod, and a word about the weather. They slid it into a bag, a pocket, the basket of a bicycle. The Teague boy, Cormac, with the baby on his hip, looked at the first page for perhaps four seconds, and I saw his lips move on the words *better than one in five,* and his shoulders came down by about an inch. He put the packet in the stroller. He said, "Thank you, Mrs. Marsh." He went out.

Pieter Vance did not read it at all. A woman I did not know turned to the second page, looked at it, and turned back. Four or five people on the way out said to me, kindly, "Good outlook, Tamsin," and one old man said it was the best-written thing the Cooperative had printed in years.

Dellen came at noon.

I had not seen him since the strip. He had not been at the Thursday, or at Marit's, or at the feed store when I had gone in for a sack of bonemeal, where I had been served by his sister-in-law with every courtesy and none of the usual remarks. He came in with his hat in his hand and nodded to Hettie. He took a packet from the stack. He did not put it away.

He read the first page standing at the counter, with his weight on one foot. Then he turned it. I saw him find the second, the sheet, with its twelve and its interval and its dead-pool date set out in the Almanac's clear flat type. He read it from top to bottom. He read the footer. He did it as I have seen a man read a bill, with a steady dull attention, taking in each line in its turn. When he had finished he looked up and found me at the end of the counter, among the scales and the string, and his face did not change.

He folded the whole packet in thirds. He did it with great care, running his thumbnail down the creases. Then he put it into the pocket of his shirt, over his heart, and buttoned the flap.

"Morning, Hettie," he said.

"Morning, Dellen."

"Thank you for the paper."

"It's a good one," said Hettie, and did not look at me, and did not need to.
