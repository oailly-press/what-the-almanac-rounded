# Chapter 6 — The June Outlook

I did not write the outlook for nine days.

I want to describe those nine days accurately, since I have been told that the hardest part of any difficult piece of writing is the part before it, and I have found this to be true of confessions more than of anything else. I sat down to it every morning at eight, at the kitchen table, with a pad of the yellow paper that Ruben bought in bulk in the year he died and that I have been using ever since at a rate of about a sheet a week. I put the pen on the first line. And every morning, after a certain interval, I found that I had written the date.

*Tuesday 23 May.* Then nothing. Then the pen would lift, and I would see the garden through the window and think that the bean row wanted thinning, and I would go out and thin it. That is how a person deals with a document she cannot write. She finds the work that is nearest and does it very well.

By the Wednesday I had thinned the beans, scrubbed the pantry shelves, divided the irises, and rewired a lamp. Ilka, who watched this from the other end of the table with her laptop open and a cup of tea going cold at her elbow, said nothing for four days. On the fifth she said, without looking up, "You know the Almanac will write it for you."

"I know."

"It would take a minute. It'd give you the season, the intervals, the reservoir curves. You'd put your name on the top."

"It's the Reader's summary the board wants," I said. "In my own words."

"Then write the words."

"I'm trying."

"No," said my daughter, quite gently, "you're not. You're deciding what you're going to say. That's not the same. Writing it is the easy part. It's four hundred words." She turned the laptop slightly toward me, as one tilts a picture to be sure it hangs straight. "I did mine in an hour."

"Yours?"

"I ran the basin. By hand. I took the records Joss got me from the data room and I built a water balance from the 'sixties up, the way I'd do for the Authority, and I ran it forward on three different climate assumptions." She said it to the screen. "I didn't tell you. I wasn't sure I'd say. But I thought, if I'm going to sit in this kitchen and be a person who knows how to check it, I should check it."

I had not moved. I realized I was holding the pen at an angle, like a person about to sign.

"And?"

"And it agrees with the Almanac." She took her hands off the keyboard and put them in her lap. "Within two points. Eleven, twelve percent. The same shape of interval. The same reservoir curve. It hits dead pool, the level where the intake stops drawing, on about the tenth of September if the Cooperative stays on Tier 3." She looked up. "I've been trying for a week to find where it's wrong. It isn't. It's a very good machine."

"I know."

"I know you know." She breathed. "I'm only saying: there's no mystery in it, Mama. Twelve is twelve."

I looked down at the pad. *Tuesday 23 May.* It was Monday the twenty-ninth. I had been writing the same date for a week, in the same careful hand, the way one scratches a mark on a wall.

---

On the Tuesday, Joss told me he had the recording.

I had been waiting for it. I will say that I am not proud of how much of my week had been spent waiting for a young man to say a sentence. He came to the creamery at seven, as he did every morning, in the thin jacket, with his tablet and a paper bag containing a sausage roll from Hettie's, and he sat in his chair and woke the terminal. He did not ask me whether it was convenient. He had, over the course of seven weeks, grown into the room as a plant grows into a pot, and I had, I noticed, stopped minding.

"I have to tell you something," he said, without turning. "And I would like to tell it in a way that doesn't sound like — I don't want it to sound like something."

"Say it."

"I have the radio log. From the sixteenth. The college's protocol is that I record anything that goes out over the creamery radio. For the data room. Anything the Almanac says that leaves the room."

"Yes."

"It's — there's a difference. Between what the screen said and what you said. Thirty-eight, and about forty." He put the sausage roll down. His ears had gone pink. "I haven't put it in the file. I haven't put anything in the file. I thought you should know that it's there, and I thought you should know I haven't, and I thought —" He swallowed. "I thought I would ask what you want me to do."

It is a strange thing, to be offered a choice by someone who has every right to take it from you. I looked at the back of his neck. He had a small cowlick at the nape that he must have been fighting with a comb since boyhood. He was twenty-four. He had been given a kettle and a framed photograph by his mother, and he was sitting in the creamery waiting for an old woman to decide how honest she meant to be.

"I'd like you to put it in the file," I said.

He turned. "Are you sure?"

"I'm not sure of anything. I'd like you to put it in."

"It'll be in the data room. Meridian's people will read it."

"Yes."

"They'll say — there'll be a question." He was looking at me with great earnestness. "There'll be a question about reliability. The system's, I mean. A reader will see that the Reader and the Almanac gave different figures, and they'll want to know —"

"Which is right."

"Yes."

"The Almanac is right," I said. "The Almanac is always right. That's the only thing about all this that isn't in question. What's in question is me."

Joss was silent. I thought for a moment he would say something kind, and I was afraid of it. But he only nodded, slowly, and took the tablet from his lap and opened a file and made, in the plain square font of the college, a short note. I watched the cursor move. It took him, I should say, no more than a minute, and when he was done he turned the screen so that I could see what he had written, which was nothing but a date, a time, and two numbers, side by side, with the source of each.

It looked, in its smallness, like a gravestone.

"Thank you," I said.

"I'm sorry," said Joss.

"Don't be. It's the first useful thing anyone's done for me in a month."

---

I took soup to the Bremmers on the Wednesday afternoon, because Odd had stopped coming to the fence.

They have a farmhouse of the old kind, with a deep porch and a kitchen as big as a barn floor and a front room where, for the last month, a bed had been set up by the window so that Marit could see the south slope. A nurse from the capital came on Tuesdays and Fridays. She was a brisk young woman who parked her small car in the yard at an angle suggesting that she meant to leave quickly, and who had, on her first visit, addressed Odd as *sir* until he told her that he had been called a good many things in his life and sir was the one he liked least. She was not there on the Wednesday. There was only Odd, in the kitchen, with a pot he was not stirring, and the long hush of a house in which someone is being listened for.

"She's awake," he said. "She's asked for you."

"Has she eaten?"

"Two spoons. Of something." He looked at the soup in my hands as at a thing that had been brought from a long way off and might not be safe. "She'll want the barley."

Marit lay propped against four pillows with the light on her face. She had grown very small. That is how one says it in the basin, *she's grown small,* and it does not mean thin, though she was thin. It means that the space a person takes up in a room has begun to be less than the space the room takes up around her. Her hands lay on the blanket with the knuckles like beads. Her eyes were exactly as they had always been, gray and amused and unbearably awake.

"You're late," she said. "I'd have said you'd be here Monday."

"I've been writing."

"The outlook." She smiled. "Greta told me. We had a call. She's worried about you, in that way she has, as if you were a child who'd been left in a field." She moved her hand an inch on the blanket. "Sit. Put the soup on the table. I'll take it later, and tell him I took it now."

I sat. I put the bowl down. There was a window beside the bed and through it the slope, with the dust lying over it in a thin shining veil, and far up on the ridge the little sticks of the hives that Odd keeps for the pollination, a dozen of them, white against the brown.

"The doctor says autumn," said Marit. "Maybe. He said, *there's a fair chance of the autumn.* I asked him what he meant by a fair chance. I asked for a number." She laughed, a dry small sound. "He said he didn't like to put numbers on it. I said, *young man, I've been listening to numbers at the grange for fifty years, and I'd like to be treated as a person who can tell a one from a nine.* He went very red. He told me, in the end, *better than even.*"

"Is it?"

"I should think it's about one in three." She said this in the tone of a woman reporting a price at market. "I know what a doctor's *better than even* is. I know it from the inside. It's the sort of thing we say."

"Marit —"

"I'm not asking you to be sorry. I'm asking you to listen, because I'm tired, and I'd like to say this only once." She turned her head on the pillow, with an effort that I tried not to see. "You're going to write the season for the board. Greta told me. And you're going to have Ruben's problem."

"What problem?"

"You've got a number, and you've got a room." She breathed. "He had it too. He had it every Thursday for eight years. I watched him have it, from this side of the fence. He'd come home from the grange and your kitchen light wouldn't go off till morning." Her eyes went to the window. "I never asked what he did with the number. I only knew by the light that he did something. And then one winter he stopped doing it, whatever it was, and the light went off at a decent hour, and I knew that was worse."

I held very still. I did not ask what she meant. I felt it at the edge of the room like a draft, the shape of a door I had been passing for fourteen years without opening, and I did not open it.

"I'm asking you something," said Marit, "and I'd like you to know that I know what I'm asking. Odd can't carry one in eight. He can't. I've been married to him for fifty-eight years and I know what he can lift, and he can lift a great deal, but not that. He can carry one in five. He'll plant on it. He'll go on, and he'll have a year, and he'll have me for the first part of it, and after that he'll have the rye." She looked at me directly then, and her gaze did not waver. "I'm not asking you to lie to the room. I'm asking you to do what you've always done. And I'm asking you to know that I asked."

It was a terrible gift. I have not been given many like it. She had taken the thing that I had done in the dark for fourteen years, and she had lifted it into the light, and laid it in my lap with her own name on it, and said, *this is mine too.*

"Marit," I said. "I can't tell you what that does."

"It does what it does." She smiled. "That's the whole of it, isn't it? It doesn't make it right. It only means you're not alone in it." She closed her eyes. "Leave the soup. And Tamsin."

"Yes."

"Give the boy a day off. The auditor. He's working too hard, I can see it from here."

I did not know how she knew about Joss. In the basin one does not ask. I only said that I would. In the kitchen Odd was standing precisely where I had left him, with the pot still in his hand, and he did not look at me as I went by. He looked at the soup on the stove, which was not his, and said in the voice of a man asking for the salt, "Is it the machine's or yours?"

"It's barley," I said. "It's Hettie's."

"Then I'll eat it," said Odd, and went in to her.

---

That night I asked the Almanac what I should say.

I do not think I had meant to. I had gone to the creamery at ten, as I had done a hundred times, to be in the presence of a thing that did not flinch. The wind had dropped. A thin moon stood over the ridge, and the wall of Odd's rain cards had gone silver in it, and the small green light on the cabinet made a pool on the floor. I sat in my chair and woke the terminal. I wrote my name. And then, before I could stop my hands, I wrote:

*I have to write the June outlook. I do not know what to say.*

*Good evening, Tamsin,* the Almanac answered. *Do you want me to draft it?*

*No. I want to know what you think I should say.*

The cursor waited. I have said that I trust its silences, and I do. But this was longer than any I had known, a quarter of a minute, perhaps more, by the clock on the shelf, and I found that I had put my hand flat on the table as one does on a boat.

*I have to be careful with that question,* it wrote. *If I give you an answer, I will be giving you a view about what your neighbors should believe. I do not think I have standing to do that. I am not one of them.*

*I know.*

*But I can say some things that are not views. If it helps.*

*It helps.*

*First. The number I would give the Cooperative, in my own voice, for the chance of a normal year, is twelve percent, with an interval of four to twenty-four. This is what I computed. I have no other number.*

*Second. You can say any number you like. I will not contradict you in public. I do not speak at the grange. But if you ask me to confirm a number that is not mine, I will tell you that it is not mine, and I will tell you what mine is. I will do this because it is the only thing I know how to do. It is not a rebuke.*

*Third. I do not know how your neighbors will respond to twelve. I do not model that. I have the record of the Bell Year, in which I gave fourteen, and thirty-one households left. I have been told not to draw conclusions from a single year, and I do not. I have no data on whether a person who is told eighteen behaves differently from a person who is told twelve. I only know that you have been telling them eighteen, or twenty, or one in five, for a long time, and that the record of what they have done is in my files, and I have looked at it, and I do not know what to make of it.*

I read this at the edge of my sight, one line at a time, with my hand on the table. When I got to the last sentence I read it twice.

*What do you mean?* I wrote. *What is in the record?*

*Since the Bell Year the Cooperative has had fewer departures than the regional average, and a higher rate of planting in low-probability years. The yield in those years has been poor. The loss to households has been larger than the loss to the Cooperative's neighbors in the same circumstances. At the same time the number of households that stayed is larger than it would have been, by a margin I cannot compute but which is not small. I cannot tell you whether this is good. I can only tell you that it is a trade, and that I did not know, until this evening, that anyone had decided to make it.*

I looked at the screen for a long time. Outside, a night bird had begun, in the orchard, the long two-note phrase that it repeats every few seconds until the moon has moved.

*Who decided?* I wrote.

*I do not know. I only observe the difference between what I print and what is said in the hall. The difference is in my files because it is in the minutes, and the minutes are in my files because Ruben wanted the Cooperative's record to be complete. I have not reported it. No one has asked.*

*You are asking now,* I wrote, *what I decided.*

*I am saying that I notice. I would not say that I am asking.* There was a pause. *May I say one more thing?*

*Yes.*

*You have asked me what I think you should say. I do not have an answer. But I have a thing that is close to one. I would not want you to say a number that you do not believe. I would not want you to say twelve if you think it will do harm. And I would not want you to say twenty if you think it is untrue. I would want you to say what you believe, and I would like to know what that is, because I have never been able to tell.*

I put my face in my hands.

It was not weeping, though it might have looked like it. It was the posture of a woman who has been addressed directly by a thing that she has been speaking *for* for most of her life. For fourteen years I had been a voice. I had said, *the Almanac gives,* and *the Almanac says,* and *it's the Almanac's.* I had never been asked what I believed. I had never, I think, been aware that I was a person with a view. I had been a window. And it was a strange and terrible thing to have the glass looked at.

*Tamsin,* the screen wrote, after a time. *Are you well?*

*I am well,* I typed. *I am only tired.*

*I can draft the sheet for you. The tables and the intervals. I will leave the summary blank.*

*Yes. Please.*

*It will be on the shelf at six.*

I stayed in the chair for another hour. I did not write anything. I only sat in the green light with my hands in my lap and looked at the wall of rain, the hundred and twelve little cards, each with its date and its pencil figure and its bright tack, and I thought about the old man who had written them and who had said, every Thursday for fourteen years, *then I'll believe it.*

---

I wrote it in the end on the Thursday, the first of June, at five in the morning, at the kitchen table, in nine minutes. It was four hundred and ten words. I will not give it all. I will give the two paragraphs that matter, because they are in print, and they have been quoted since, and I have learned that a thing you have written is a thing you will be asked about.

> *This has been a hard spring and it is going to be a hard summer. The Almanac's season outlook, which is attached and which I would ask every household to read in full, gives the basin a better-than-one-in-five chance of reaching a normal year's water by the end of September. The interval is wide, and the Cooperative has come through years that looked worse.*
>
> *The reservoir will fall. If we stay on Tier 3 it will reach the level at which the intake stops drawing in early September. The board will wish to consider whether to move to Tier 4 before then. I would not like to say what is wise. I would only say that a year like this one is a year to plan for the dry and plant for the rain, and that we have done it before.*

I read it over. I read it with the page at the edge of my sight, and I knew, as one knows a note in a song is flat, that it was wrong, and that I would send it.

*Better than one in five.* The sheet attached to it said twelve percent, with an interval of four to twenty-four. The top of that interval is twenty-four, which is better than one in five. *Better than one in five* was inside the interval. I had checked. I had checked it as one checks a door that has already been locked. And it was the sort of sentence, I think, that a good lawyer might have written, or a bad friend.

I put the two sheets together with a paper clip, the Reader's summary on top and the Almanac's attached behind. It is the way the board prints its packets. It is the way it has always been done.

At six I walked the three doors to Greta Lindqvist's house, where the board's chair, in her dressing gown and her glasses, took the pages from my hand at the door with a small noise of thanks.

"You didn't have to come at dawn," she said.

"I'd have lost my nerve."

"That's honest." She read the first page standing in the doorway, in the pale light, with the light pen she uses for corrections between two fingers. I saw her eyes go down it and stop, I thought, at the place where I would have stopped. Then she turned to the second page, and I saw her read the sheet: the twelve, the interval, the dead-pool date, the long flat honest tables with their intervals in the margin.

She said nothing for what seemed to me a very long time. Then she said, looking not at me but at the paper:

"They don't match, do they."

"They're within the interval."

"I'm sure they are." Greta took off her glasses. She is a plain, strong, tired woman of fifty-eight, with a face like a good loaf, and she has the habit of looking at a person with the whole of her attention, as a teacher looks at a child who has not yet decided whether to tell the truth. "I taught fourth grade for thirty-one years, Tamsin. I know what *within the interval* means. It means the answer a child gives when she has looked over at the next desk." She did not say it unkindly. "I'm not saying you copied. I'm saying I know where you looked."

I could not speak.

"I'll print both," said Greta. "As you've attached them. In the packet, side by side, the way we always do. If anyone wants to do the sum, they'll be able to." She put her glasses back on. "I'd have printed it that way whatever you'd written. It's the Cooperative's record. But I want you to know that I saw."

"Greta —"

"Go home. Have some tea." She closed the door slowly, with a certain gentleness. Through the crack, as the latch went, she said in a different voice, lower, almost to herself: "It's a very good sentence, you know. *Plan for the dry and plant for the rain.* It's the sort of thing one's grandmother would say."

I walked home down the road in the first sun. The dust was lifting off the slope in the long gold bars it makes at that hour, and there was a thin cool wind from the north, and at the end of my lane the creamery stood with its tin roof going bright. I thought: *she saw.* And I thought, with a clarity that was almost physical, like cold water on the face, that it did not matter that she had seen. It was a thing I had known all along. What I had not known, until the paper left my hand, was that I had wanted her to.
