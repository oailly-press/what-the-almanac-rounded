# Chapter 16 — Nine Percent

On the nineteenth of October, a Thursday, I stood at the lectern for the first time in two months, and read the Note.

I had not planned to. The rota gave the evening to Brigid Teague, who read the sheet in a small, fast, fierce voice, like a woman threading a needle in a high wind, and who said *nine* as if she were defying it. But when she had finished and said *This is mine* and told us, shakily, that she had been out at the Hollin gauge at six that morning and the dew point had been thirteen point one, and the morning before thirteen point four, and that she did not think she had ever been so frightened of a number that was going up, Greta had looked down the hall and said, "Reader of Record. If you'd like."

I would not have said that I wanted to. I went up all the same.

The sheet that Brigid had read was on the lectern. I looked at it once more, for the form of it. *Next seven days: chance of a wetting rain, twelve millimeters or more, basin average: nine percent. Interval: five to fifteen.* The Almanac had added a note, which was in the footer, in the same small type as everything else. *The dip in the upper-level flow has deepened. It is now centered south-west of the coast. This is the pattern I have associated with the autumn rains in nine of sixty-one years. I give it nine percent because the pattern does not guarantee the rain; it only makes it possible.*

"Nine percent," I said. "That's the Almanac's. It's the highest it's been since the middle of August. It's up from seven." I took off my glasses, and found that I did not need them. The gray place at the center of things had spread a little that month, but the light in the grange was good, and I had learned to read the lines by their edges. "This is mine."

I had thought a great deal about what I would say. I had drafted it in the creamery in four versions. I found that I had not brought any of them.

"I've a hive," I said. "It's the third in the second row, on the south slope. It's the one the boy counts. In April it was a strong colony, seventy-one bees going in and sixty-two coming out in thirty seconds. In July it was fifty. In September, forty. This morning I counted it myself, with Wren, from the same stone." I looked at the page. "It was twenty-two in. Nineteen out."

There was no sound.

"That's low," I said. "I've kept bees for thirty years, and I can tell you that a hive that's gone that quiet has heard something. I'd not say it's rain. I'd not say that. Hives go quiet for a great many reasons, and one of them is that they're afraid of the cold. But I'd say that it's the lowest I've known them since a day in the Bell Year. And I've read the Almanac's third sign, and it says *the bees are low,* and I think —" I stopped. I looked out at the hundred and sixty faces, at Hettie in the third seat, at Dellen against the wall. "I think I'd bring the washing in."

Somebody laughed. It was not a large laugh. It was a short, soft, surprised one, and it came from the back, where the Fenns stood, and then it was in the middle rows, and then the whole hall was laughing, and I saw Greta put her pen down and cover her mouth. It was the laugh of people who have been very afraid for a long time and have just been told that they are allowed to be a little silly.

"That's mine," I said, "and I don't know what it means. Goodnight."

I came down from the lectern. As I passed the front row, Odd, who had been brought in a chair by Dellen and set at the end with a blanket across his knees, put out his hand and caught my sleeve.

"Washing," he said. "I'd have said you'd round it up to *bring in the sheets.*"

"I'd have said sheets," I said, "a month ago."

"Aye." He smiled. "It's better."

---

It began at twelve minutes past three.

I am sure of the time. I have the Almanac's log, and Joss's, and a boy's, and they agree. I had gone to bed at ten. I had not slept well; I had lain for a long time listening to the house, in the way one listens to a thing one has stopped expecting to hear, and then at some point I had slept, and then there was a sound.

It was very small. It was a single, faint, distinct *tick* against the window glass. I lay quite still. After a long moment there was another. Then three together, and then a space, and then a scatter, like handfuls of dry rice thrown against a pane, and under it, almost too low to hear, a kind of whispering that I could not identify for a moment, because I had not heard it in a hundred and fifty-seven days.

I got up. I did not turn on the light. I went down in my nightdress and my husband's old coat and opened the kitchen door, and stood on the step.

It was dark, and warm, and the air smelled of something I had forgotten. I cannot describe it to anyone who has not been parched. It is a smell like the inside of a clay jar, like hot stones being poured over with cold water, like the first moment of turned earth; and it came up from the whole of the basin at once, from the roofs and the road and the dust of the lane, in a long, slow, sweet, mineral wave, so that I put my hand over my mouth, and found that I was weeping, and had not noticed starting.

It was raining.

It was not much. It was a thin, soft, steady, unhurried rain, falling straight down in the dark with no wind in it, drop after drop onto the tin of the creamery roof, so that the whole long shed began to speak in a low continuous drumming, a sound like a thousand small thoughtful fingers. It made the dust of the yard jump. It made small dark pocks in it, and then the pocks ran together, and the road shone. In the orchard the pear leaves began to tick. From the hives, a hundred yards off, came a sound that I had been waiting all my life to hear and had not known: not the low anxious hum of a colony that has understood, but a high, bright, rising chord, like a crowd that has just been told a piece of news.

A light came on behind me. A door banged. Wren came out of the house at a run in his pajamas and his green sweater and a pair of Ilka's rubber boots, with a flashlight in one hand and the notebook under his arm, and went straight past me down the steps, through the rain, to the fence post at the foot of the garden where the first jar stood with its taped ruler.

"It's not six!" he shouted over his shoulder.

"No."

"Is it a reading if it's not six?"

"The Almanac will want it," said a voice.

My daughter was standing in the doorway behind me in a flannel nightgown and the field jacket, with her hair on end. She had her hand flat on the frame. She was looking at the rain, with an expression that I had never seen on her face, and that I realized, slowly, was the look of a person watching a thing that she has predicted come true, and finding that the sensation was not at all what she had supposed.

"I said fifteen years," said Ilka. "I said it was going to run out."

"It may."

"I know." She took a step out onto the stone, and then another, into the wet, and stood with her face lifted and her arms a little away from her sides, in the old flat posture of a field hydrologist at a gauge. "It will. But I've never —" She laughed, short and helpless. "I've never been in it, when it was a *nine.*"

"Zero point four!" shouted Wren, from the garden. His voice cracked on it, thin and high with joy. "It's zero point *four,* Grandma. It's not zero. It's not zero!"

---

By four there were lights in every house in the basin.

I know because I saw them. I had walked down the lane to the creamery, in the coat, with my head bare, and the rain had come on a little heavier by then, a good steady rain, an inch of water standing in the ruts, and from the step of the creamery I could see the whole of Carrow laid out below me in the dark: a thousand small yellow windows, scattered over the slope like spilled coins, and every one of them lit. I have never seen the basin so awake. People were out in their yards. They stood in the rain in their nightclothes with their faces turned up, as I had seen children stand in the first snow of a winter, in a kind of grave, bewildered, mute rapture. Along the road, on every fence post, a small white torch was moving. I thought for a moment that it was a procession. Then I understood.

They were reading their gauges.

Joss was at the creamery. I do not know how he had got there before me. He was in the second chair in a coat over his pajamas, with his hair standing straight up, and he had both hands flat on the shelf on either side of the terminal like a man at the controls of a ship, and he did not look round when I came in.

"It's coming up from the south-west," he said. "The cell's about forty kilometers across. It's not intense. It's — there's a band. It's very — *look.*"

I looked. On the screen, in the Almanac's plain gray type, there was a small gray map of the basin with the radar returns laid over it in a soft green wash. It was a very modest-looking thing. It showed a broad, slow, pale band of rain moving up from the lower left corner toward the center, and along its leading edge a scatter of tiny white numbers, and the numbers were changing as I watched. *0.6. 0.8. 0.5. 1.2. 0.9.*

"Those are the gauges," said Joss. "The children's. They're phoning them in to Hettie's. She's got the telephone off the hook and a boy on a bicycle, and she's relaying them to the creamery by the old radio, and I'm typing them in. By *hand.* I've done a hundred and three." He drew a ragged breath. "The Almanac's — it's *using* them. Look."

*Good morning, Tamsin,* said the screen. *It is 04:07. Rain is falling at 111 of 112 reporting gauges. The amounts range from 0.3 mm on the lower terraces to 2.1 mm on the Fenn terrace. I have not had this density of observation in sixty-one years. I can tell you, for the first time, where the rain is falling, and I can tell you at what rate it is moving. The band will pass over the basin from the south-west between 04:30 and 07:00. I expect the total at the creamery to be between four and nine millimeters.*

*It was nine percent,* I typed.

*Yes. I said so.*

*Are you —* I stopped. I did not know what to ask. I sat with my hands over the keys and the rain coming down on the tin above us, a steady, thorough, cheerful sound, and the little white numbers going on and off on the map like fireflies.

*Please take no notice of what I am about to say,* the Almanac wrote. *I am aware that it is not a statement about the weather.*

*Say it.*

*The nine percent I gave was for a wetting rain, twelve millimeters or more across the basin. By my present estimate tonight will fall short of that. I want that said before anyone says the nine percent has come true. What has happened is that the three signs were right, and that the sky has done part of what they permit. If I give nine percent every time the signs are as they have been this week, the full rain should come about one time in eleven. That is the meaning of nine percent. It is a statement about frequency. It does not mean the other ten were wrong, and it will not mean I was right if the gauges pass twelve by morning. It means only that the number was honest.*

*I have noticed,* it added, *that I would not have said this a year ago. I would have said nothing. I find that I want it to be on the record that a nine percent is not a zero. It seems to me the most important thing I know.*

I read that. I read it several times, around the gray place. I could hear Joss breathing beside me. I could hear the rain.

*It is on the record,* I typed.

*Thank you,* said the Almanac. *I will keep the readings coming in. I would like to go on, if I may. The map is very beautiful.*

---

I went to Odd's at half past six.

I did not know that I was going. I had left the creamery in the first gray of the dawn, with the rain still falling, lighter now, and had meant to go home and light the stove. But my feet took the other track, the one that runs between my hay and his rye, and I found myself walking it, with the rain on my face, in the long gray light, past the ditch that had begun, I saw, to run. It was a thin, brown, hesitant trickle. It ran down the middle of the dry channel in a series of small, careful, muddy tongues, and it chattered over the stones. A moth was on the water. It was an ordinary moth, a drab one, and it rode the current for a yard, turning slowly, until a stone took it.

The rye lay silver. I do not mean that it had come up. It was the stubble of the old crop and the dry, thin, newly planted rows of the winter sowing, which Odd had put in on the first of October against all the sense of the thing, and the rain had stood on them in a million small beads, so that the whole slope shone like a dish of mercury. I stood at the edge of the field and looked at it for some time. It was a thing I had not thought to see.

He was in the yard.

He was standing by the fence post, in the rain, with his canvas coat open and no hat on, and his white hair plastered flat to his skull. He was holding the gauge in both hands, the old brass one that had been his father's, and he had tipped it very carefully toward the gray light, with one eye shut, and was reading it against the little engraved scale. I came up behind him without a sound. I do not think he heard. He stood there for a long moment, quite still, an old man in a wet coat, with the rain running down the back of his neck.

"Nine point two," said Odd.

He said it quietly, to no one. He lowered the gauge. He turned, and saw me, and I saw that his whole face was wet and that most of it was not from the sky.

"Nine point two millimeters," he said. "I'd have said six. I'd have bet it on the rye." He stopped, and looked down at the gauge, and I realized that he was shaking, from head to foot, in a long continuous fine tremor like the shiver of a horse. "It's the first one I've been *given.* In sixty-one years. The first one I've been told. I've had a thousand. I've had a thousand that came when I'd been told it wouldn't, and a thousand that didn't, and nobody ever — nobody said *nine.* They said *maybe.* They said *it'll come.* They said *don't worry.* And this one, they *said* it. Nine. And it's come." He laughed. It was a thin cracked, ecstatic sound. "Isn't it a thing. It's lovely."

"Odd, come in. You'll take cold."

"I'll not." He put his hand out and took my sleeve, and for a moment we stood there in the rain, and I understood that he was holding on. "I'll need a slip. I'd like to write it. I've got an envelope in the house. Tamsin, you'll write it for me. I can't hold the pencil."

I took him in. He went up the porch steps on my arm, one at a time, with the rain streaming off him. In the kitchen, in the dim gold light of the lamp that had been burning all night, I sat him in his chair and found a stub of pencil in the left pocket of the coat, and a slip, and an envelope, and a drawer of them, in the study, where the lamp was still on over the desk and the banks of little drawers stood in their rows to the ceiling.

I wrote what he told me. I put the date. I put the number, *9.2.* I put it in the same square pencil hand, because he had told me to, and it came out looking, I thought, rather like his.

"The word," said Odd.

"What word?"

"There's always a word." He leaned back. His eyes were closed. His face, in the lamplight, had gone very quiet and composed, with the look of a man at the end of a long working day. "What's it *for?* I've never put one that didn't mean something. You'd write —"

"I'm not sure."

"*Come,*" said Odd.

"Come?"

"It came." He smiled. "It said it might. And it came."

I wrote it. *Come.* I folded the slip and slid it into the envelope, and wrote the date on the front, as he had taught me, in pencil, and carried it into the study. I found the drawer for the year. It was the last of the lowest bank. It was nearly empty. I slid the envelope into the row, at the very end, beside the one from the day before, which said *0.0.* and had no word.

When I came back, he was asleep. He was sleeping with his head against the chair back and his hands loose on his knees, in the yellow light, with the rain going on and on against the window. The kitchen was warm. I sat down at the table and watched him. After a time I became aware of a sound in the house, and I realized that it was the sound of his breathing, deep and slow and even, the breathing of someone who had set the weight down, and I sat with him until the nurse came.

He lived nine more days.

---

It rained until a quarter past nine. When the cloud broke, there was a long bright washed light over the basin, and the whole slope steamed. The creek on Tern Terrace ran for eleven days, a small, brisk, brown, loud thing, loud enough to hear from the road. The reservoir took it in. It came up two points, from twenty-six to twenty-eight, which is not much. It is not a thing that a farmer would call a rain. It did not save the barley. It did not save the hay, or the hives that I had lost, or the year, which was gone and would not come back. But the winter rye that Odd had sown on the first of October, against every reasonable expectation, came up in the first week of November in a thin green haze over the whole of the south slope, as it does in a good year, and the children walked out to look at it, in a long straggling line, with their rulers.

At the post office, on the afternoon of the twentieth, Hettie wrote the figures in the green book. She had done it, she told me, with the pencil in her fist and the canary shrilling at the sill, in a column of a hundred and twelve numbers. It was the first time that she had written anything in that column other than a zero. The lowest was 3.1. The highest was 11.2, on the Fenn terrace. The mean, which Joss computed that evening on the back of a sheet of the college's green paper, in his fine slanting hand, was 6.4.

"It's a very good distribution," said Joss. He said it with the air of a man announcing a birth. "It's got a long tail. I've never seen so clean a —"

"Joss."

"Yes?"

"I'd go to bed."

"I will," said Joss. He took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes. He was crying again, in the way that he cried, helplessly and without embarrassment. "I'll just. I'll just finish the map."

---

I went to the creamery at ten that night. I do not know why I went. There was nothing I needed to ask. I turned on the lamp, and the little green light came up on the cabinet, and the wall of Odd's rain cards stood out of the dark in its rows with the tacks gleaming like a small town at evening. Some of the cards were wet. Someone had come in with a coat. On the shelf, beside the printer, between the green book and the yellow paper, lay Wren's notebook with its stiff blue cover.

I opened it to the last page.

*Reader's note:* said the page, at the foot, in a boy's careful hand. And beneath, the clean blue-ruled space, an inch of margin on either side, and the small penciled arrow in the corner.

I took out a pen. I did not hesitate. I had written the sentence in my head, I think, on the first of April, at a lectern, with a number in my hand, and had not known that I was writing.

*20 October. It rained. 6.4 mm across the basin, 9.2 at Odd's, 11.2 at the Fenns'. The Almanac said nine percent, for twelve millimeters. This was not that rain. It said so itself, at four in the morning, before anyone could call it right. Nine percent is not zero. It is not a promise, either. It is a number that a person can stand on, and that is what it is for.*

*I used to round it. I do not any more. This is mine.*

I put down the pen. I read it over, around the gray place, with the page at the edge of my sight. It did not look like much.

At the other end of the shelf, the terminal woke. I had not touched it. It does not speak unless it is spoken to; in twenty-two years it never had; and I looked at it for a moment with a certain alarm, and then I saw the cursor, and the single line on the screen.

*Good evening, Tamsin,* it said. *It is 22:11. The ten o'clock soil probe at the south slope came in a few minutes ago. Thirty centimeters: thirteen percent. It has been rising since five this morning. I wanted you to know. I did not want to print it before you had come.*

Nothing in it forbade that, Joss told me afterward, when I asked. Ruben had left it the power to speak first. It had only never, in twenty-two years, judged that it had a reason.

I sat down. I pulled the chair up to the shelf. In the green light, with the rain-cards at my back, I put my two hands on the keys, and I wrote, as I have written a hundred times, with the old stiff pleasure of a ritual that has been given back:

*This is Tamsin. Good evening.*

---

On the Thursday, the twenty-sixth, I did not read the sheet. It was Hettie's turn. She stood at the lectern in the little feathered hat, with the green book under her arm, and she read it, in a sharp clear carrying voice, every figure in its place. *Next seven days: chance of a wetting rain: eleven percent. Interval: six to eighteen.* She read the line at the foot. And then she said, without a flicker, in a tone of the most complete calm: "This is mine. It is eleven. It's up. I should say it's the highest it's been since the spring, and I should say that I've put on my second-best stockings." The hall laughed. Hettie waited. "That's all. That's mine."

I was in the third seat from the aisle on the left. It was not my own seat. It was Hettie's, and she had said that I might.

At the front, in the right-hand chair, with a blanket across his knees, sat Odd. He was very thin. He had lost the color in his face entirely and his eyes were enormous. He had asked to be carried. Dellen and Pieter Vance had done it between them, in the chair, up the steps, like a king, and had set it down in its place, and Odd had thanked them in a low scraped whisper and said that he would not have had them any other way.

When Hettie had finished, and sat down, and the room had settled, and the quiet came, the one that comes in the minute after the number, he did not ask his question. He turned in the chair, slowly, and looked back down the hall to where I sat. He did not need to say it. I saw him open his mouth, and shut it, and smile.

I knew the answer. I think he did too.

*Is that the machine's number,* the whole room asked, in its silence, *or yours?*

*Both,* I thought. *And both of us have said so.*

Outside, the light went out of the sky in a long slow bar of rose and pewter over the ridge. The dust was down. In the road, a hundred and twelve small jars stood on their posts with their taped rulers, in the dusk, each with a quarter-inch of water at the bottom, for the first time since they were set, catching what the sky had left them. It was not much. It was a number. I could have counted it with my eyes shut.
