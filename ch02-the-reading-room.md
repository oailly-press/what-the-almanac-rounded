# Chapter 2 — The Reading Room

The young man who came to audit the Almanac arrived on a Friday, on the morning bus, with a wheeled suitcase that was not made for gravel.

I saw him from the orchard. The bus stops at the head of my lane because the driver, a woman named Pell who has been doing the Carrow run since I was forty, believes that anyone who needs the creamery needs me first. He stood at the end of the lane for a time with the suitcase upright beside him and a flat bag across his shoulder, and he looked, not at the lane or at the creamery or at the long brown slope behind it, but at the sky. He looked up the way a child looks up in a church. Then he took out a handkerchief and sneezed into it, once, with exactly the economy of a person who has been told all his life that he is loud.

By the time I had put down the pruning saw and come through the gate, he had begun to walk, and the suitcase had begun to refuse. It made the sound of a small animal being asked to do something it had not agreed to. When I reached him, he had stopped and was looking at one of the wheels, which had come off, with the grave interest of a man discovering that a thing he had assumed to be a whole was an assembly.

"Mrs. Aldecott," he said, before I could speak. "I'm Joss Penhallow. I'm sorry about the wheel."

"It's not my wheel."

"No, but it's on your lane."

He was twenty-four, I learned later, though he looked both older and younger: a tall narrow person with a long Cornish-sounding name and the clean, pale, unsunned face of someone who had spent his youth in rooms with lamps. He had enormous careful hands. He held the wheel in one of them as if it were a hatchling. His hair was cut short by someone who did not know him well, and he wore a jacket a size too light for the basin in April, over a shirt buttoned to the throat, and a lanyard with a plastic card on it that said REGIONAL COLLEGE OF HYDROLOGY AND DATA — APPRENTICE in a typeface that tried to look reassuring.

"You're the board's boy," I said.

"I'm an apprentice. I'm assigned to the Cooperative through the college for the spring term and, if it goes well, the summer." He hesitated. "The board's letter said you'd been told."

"I was told on Tuesday," I said, "by Dellen Roque, in the feed store, over a sack of bone meal. I was told someone was coming to document the Almanac. For the data room."

"For the data room," Joss said. He said it with a faint wince, as if the phrase were unlovely luggage. "That's the term Meridian uses. The prospective acquirer's due diligence process. They'd like the forecasting system described. Its inputs, its performance record, its —" he looked at me — "its governance."

"Its governance."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

I picked up the suitcase. It is not a thing I do. I am a person who has stood for most of her life in front of other people's difficulties without lifting a finger, because that is how a Reader keeps her place, but there was a young man in a thin jacket who had been looking at the sky, and I had been rude to him in the first minute for a reason that had nothing to do with him. It was heavier than it looked. Inside it, I would discover, was a second suitcase, folded flat, in case he acquired things; a kettle; four notebooks; a set of small screwdrivers; and a framed photograph of his mother, who had put it there herself.

"Come and meet it," I said.

---

I have tried since to describe what the Reading Room looks like to a person seeing it for the first time, and I have learned that I can only do it by describing what the person does. They stop in the doorway. They look at the black cabinet and the single terminal and the card-covered wall, and then they look for the rest, because no one believes the thing that decides the water for two hundred and twelve households can be that small. Joss stopped. He looked. He turned his head and looked at the ceiling, and at the window, and at the floor, and then at me.

"Where's the rest of it?"

"That's it."

"It's, what, a four-rack?"

"It's the cabinet. There's a second one under the table. It gets warm in July." I put the suitcase down on the floor, where it fell over. "Ruben built the first version from salvaged parts and a model he trained on the basin's records. They've changed most of the parts since. The records are all still there. Sixty-one years of them. The basin station, the Bremmer gauges, the reservoir logs, the canal logs, the headgate counts, the old ledgers that Dellen's grandfather kept. It's not a large thing. It's a small thing that has been fed very carefully."

"Does it connect to anything?"

"No."

"It's — sorry. Does it have an outbound link at all? A satellite feed, a weather service?"

"It has a radio," I said, "that listens. A man in the capital sends us a lump of satellite and radar data every six hours, over a line we pay him for, which comes into a box by the door and is carried to the cabinet on a cable that Ruben used to call the umbilical. There's nothing that goes out. It does not have the internet. It could not tell you the news." I paused. "It could not tell you what the people of this basin will do when they hear a number, either, which is the part nobody asks about."

Joss was looking at the box by the door. There was something in his expression that I had not expected, a sort of uncomplicated pleasure, the look of someone who has been reading about a rare bird for years and has been shown, at last, its nest.

"That's so clean," he said.

"Is it?"

"It's — an air gap. It's an actual air gap. Everyone says it and nobody has one." He crouched by the box and read a label upside down. "Do you know how many commercial systems of this class I've audited? Four. Four, and every one of them has a vendor support tunnel that someone has to go and close. And this —" He stopped. "I'm sorry. I get excited."

"You're allowed."

"The college says it's unprofessional."

"The college has not been to Carrow."

He smiled then, for the first time. It was a lopsided smile, and it altered his face the way a small window alters a wall.

---

I woke the terminal for him. I did it myself, though he could have, because there are things I will not hand over until I have seen what a person does with the first of them. I typed the usual: *This is Tamsin. Good morning.*

*Good morning, Tamsin,* said the Almanac. *It is 09:12. The six o'clock reading is on the shelf.*

*There is someone here with me,* I typed. *His name is Joss Penhallow. He is from the college, on the board's instruction. He will be looking at how you work.*

*Hello, Joss,* wrote the Almanac. *You are welcome here. I should say at the start what I am able to show you and what I am not. I can show you my inputs, the record of my past readings and what followed them, my configuration, and the code and the weights that make me up. I cannot show you what I will do before I do it, and I cannot show you why in any way that is simpler than the whole of it. If you ask me a question I will answer it as accurately as I can, and I will tell you when I do not know. I would ask that you treat the words on this screen as statements and not as moods. I do not have moods. I may sound as if I do.*

Joss read this with great care and for much longer than it takes to read. He sat down in the second chair, and I saw that he was trembling, very slightly, and I understood that it was not nerves but something more like reverence. He typed, with two fingers, exactly as he must have typed a hundred queries: *Hello. May I ask you some questions about your configuration?*

*Yes.*

*Does anything in your configuration alter the probabilities you compute before you publish them?*

My own breath stopped. I will not say it stopped for long. But I will tell you I felt it, as you feel a stair that is not there, and I did what I had trained myself to do for fourteen years, which is that I did not move.

*Yes,* said the Almanac. *There are three kinds of adjustment. The first is calibration: after each season I compare my stated odds with what happened, and where I have been systematically too high or too low I correct for it. The second is blending: I combine several internal estimates, and the weights of that blend are part of my configuration. The third is a set of presentation parameters, which govern how I round and what I print. Do you want me to list them?*

*Please,* Joss typed.

I will give you the list as the screen gave it, since it is the first time I saw it written out for a stranger.

> PRESENTATION PARAMETERS (current)
> rounding_step_percent: 1
> interval_level: 0.80
> publish_interval: true
> publish_unrounded_in_footer: true
> footer_font_pt: 7
> as_quoted_marker: true
> courage: 0.00

Joss read it down. I watched him read it. He was a quick reader, and I saw his eye pause and move on, once, at the last line, the way a person's eye moves on at a hole in the road.

"What's the footer?" he said aloud. He turned to me, and I understood that I was supposed to be the one to explain, which is the way it has always been.

"The weekly sheet," I said. "Hettie posts a printed sheet at the post office every Friday. It's got the week's morning and evening readings on it in a table. At the bottom, in small print, there's the unrounded values and the intervals. It's a legal requirement of the Cooperative. Article 14."

"Seven-point," Joss said. "That's very small."

"It's what Ruben thought was fair."

"Does anyone read it?"

"Hettie."

He looked at me for a moment. Then he laughed, one short unwilling syllable, and put the back of his hand to his mouth. "I'm sorry," he said. "It's just — I've been in a lot of governance reviews. It is extremely unusual for a thing to be disclosed this thoroughly and read this little."

"It's the only way," I said, "that anything is ever disclosed."

I will say, in the interest of what I said I would do, that I did not tell him about the last line. I did not say, *you will want to look at that, but not today.* I looked past it as he had done, and I took from the shelf the six o'clock sheet and put it in his hand, and I said: "The thing I would like you to understand, Mr. Penhallow, before you start on the details, is what the Almanac is for. It's not to tell anyone what to do. It's to tell them what is likely, so that they can decide, and then to tell them afterward whether it was right. That's all. It isn't a priest."

"No," Joss said. "I can see that."

"I'm not sure you can," I said, "yet." And I was surprised, hearing it, to find that I meant it kindly.

---

We took lunch at my table, which is a thing I had not planned. There was soup. There was the heel of a loaf that Marit had sent over on Tuesday with a cloth over it, and some of Odd's rye that I toast in a pan because the oven has been broken since the winter. Joss ate the way young men do who have been told to put on weight and have taken it as a challenge, and for the first ten minutes he said nothing, and I let him. I am fond of the sound of a person eating. It is one of the few true sounds.

"Can I ask you something that isn't about the system?" he said, finally.

"You may ask."

"Why is it called the Almanac? It's not an almanac. An almanac is a — it's predictions for the whole year, printed in advance. This is evening and morning. It's closer to a bulletin."

"Ruben named it," I said. "He said an almanac was what a farmer used to consult before he decided to believe it. He said the difference between an almanac and a forecast was that you could argue with an almanac."

"Can you argue with it?"

"You can ask it to explain. It will explain at length. It has never once been persuaded."

Joss considered this. He had finished the soup and was running a piece of toasted rye around the bowl with a precision that made me think of drafting.

"The college's file says the Cooperative had a — I'm not sure how to put it. A crisis, in its early years. The Bell Year."

I had expected this and had not expected it so soon. I put down my spoon.

"The file is the board's," I said. "You'll be given it. I'd rather you read it than I summarized it. I wasn't on the board for any of it, and the part I lived isn't the part you'll need."

"But you were here."

"I was here," I said, "and my husband was the Reader, and I was sitting in the third row at the grange in the autumn of the Bell Year when the Almanac said fourteen percent."

"Fourteen percent of what?"

"A normal year." I watched the toast go round the bowl. "It was right. It was the right number. It said fourteen and the number was right, and the rains didn't come, and I have never been able to decide what to do with the fact that the number was right."

Joss did not say anything. He did a thing I have since seen him do many times and have come to rely on: he put down whatever he was holding, and he waited.

"Thirty-one households left that winter," I said. "That was nearly a sixth of the Cooperative. They sold their land and their rights, mostly to the first buyer who came. Some of them would have been all right. That's the thing. Some would have been entirely all right, if they'd stayed. They'd have taken the loss and borrowed against the next year and come out. But they heard *fourteen*, in the third row, in a room where everyone else was hearing it, and what they heard was the number that everyone else heard, and the thing about a number everyone hears is that you can't be the one who stays." I stopped. "I'm not explaining it well."

"No," Joss said. "You are."

The wind had come up while I talked. It does, around noon, in spring. It came down the south slope and across the yard and laid the whole orchard over, and the first of the peach blossom went by the window sideways like a fall of small pink coins.

"I'd like to ask you something," I said. "Since you're going to be asking me things."

"Yes."

"If a person knows a true number, and the number will hurt, and the person says it in a way that makes it hurt less, is that a lie?"

I had not meant to ask it. It came out the way a stone comes out of the ground in a plowed field, because the plow has gone a little deeper than usual.

Joss looked at me with a degree of attention that I had not met in a long time. I realize now that he was weighing the question as if it were a technical one.

"In my field," he said, "it depends on whether the person has an obligation to calibrate. If they've *said* they'll give you the best estimate, then yes. If they've said they'll give you something to plan on, then — no. Not necessarily. It's a different thing. Forecasters call it *wishcasting* when you move a number toward the one you hope for. But there's a long tradition of *hedging*, of putting the number in a form the listener can use." He rubbed his thumb along the table's edge. "I don't think I've ever heard anyone say it was kind."

"I didn't say it was kind."

"No," said Joss, gently. "I'm sorry. I meant — I've never heard anyone say it was *right*."

There is a thing that happens when someone says a true sentence in your kitchen, and it is not what you think. It is not shame. It is a kind of sudden thinness of the air, as if a window had been opened in a room you had not known had a wall.

"The soup's gone cold," I said, and took his bowl.

---

The telephone rang at four. It is a wall telephone, a tan one with a cord, that Ruben wired in when the mobile towers went down for the second time in a single winter and he decided that there should be at least one thing in the house that did not need a signal. I was in the garden, taking the dead heads off the last of the hellebores. Joss was in the Reading Room, working, and I had been letting myself listen for the printer, whose rhythm is as familiar to me as the breathing of someone in the next room.

I knew who it was before I reached the receiver. There are certain people in whom the telephone goes quiet in a particular way.

"Mama."

"Ilka."

"I wanted to tell you before you heard it from Dellen." There was wind on the line, and the long wash of a bus or a train. My daughter has been in the habit, since she was nineteen, of calling me from places that are in motion. "I'm coming home. Not for good. For the spring and the first part of the summer. I'm taking leave from the Authority."

"Is something wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." That was the first thing she said that was not quite true, and I heard it, and she heard that I heard it, and neither of us said so. We have always been good at that, my daughter and I. We are two people who have learned to conduct whole arguments in the interval between the sentences. "I'm bringing Wren. He's out of school in two weeks and he's been asking about the bees."

"The bees are fine."

"I know they're fine. He's nine. He's asking about them like they might not be."

I put the dead heads in my apron pocket, one at a time. I have a habit, when a conversation unsettles me, of finishing whatever small task is in my hands, and I did it now with the care of a person defusing something.

"Ilka," I said. "Why now?"

Across the line I heard her breathe, a very particular one, in through the nose. She did that as a child before saying the true part.

"There's a — I've been asked to consult on the Carrow sale," she said. "Meridian's people asked the Authority for a technical review of the water rights, and my name came up. I said no. I want you to know that I said no, Mama. I said it was a conflict. I said it was my mother's basin."

"And?"

"And then I thought: if someone's going to read the numbers in Carrow, I'd rather it was someone who —"

"Someone who what?"

"Who loves it," she said, in a voice that I did not know she had. "I don't know. Someone who'd read them honestly."

I did not answer, and this time the not-answering was heavy enough to hear.

"I'm coming on the twenty-fifth," Ilka said. "Wren wants to know if you still have the blue bike."

"It's in the barn."

"He'll want to know if it's got air."

"I'll put air in it."

She laughed, once, the short laugh of a daughter who has been given a small mercy and does not want to look at it. "Thank you, Mama. I'll — we'll talk. We'll sit down and we'll talk about all of it."

"Yes."

"I love you."

"Yes," I said. "You too."

I hung the receiver on its hook. The cord swung. Out the window I could see the creamery, and the small gray figure of Joss on the step with a notebook on his knee, doing a thing with his hands that I eventually understood to be tapping out the rhythm of the printer on his thigh.

*Someone who'd read them honestly.* I stood there with my apron full of dead hellebores and the word going round and round in the thin air of my kitchen, like the moth on the screen: *honestly, honestly*. It is not a word one expects to be hit by. It is a word for other people.

---

Hettie posted the weekly sheet at five.

I did not go. I rarely do. But Joss did, because I had told him that if he wanted to understand the Cooperative he should stand in front of the corkboard on a Friday at five and watch the people who did not look at it. So he walked the three doors down, in his light jacket, with one hand over his nose against the dust, and I am told by Hettie that he stood in front of the sheet for a long time with his head tilted, and that he read the whole thing, table and footer, in the posture of a man reading a headstone.

I was told this in the evening, at my gate, by Hettie herself, who had walked down the lane in the dusk with a covered dish that I knew to be nothing but an excuse.

"He read the small print," she said.

"I asked him to."

"No, I mean he read it. All of it. He took out a pencil." She said this with an expression I could not place; it was not disapproval, and it was not triumph. It was something more like the face of a woman who has kept a very small light burning in a window for fourteen years and has just seen someone, at last, walk up the road toward it. "He asked me whether I'd ever had anyone else ask. About the footer."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him no," said Hettie. "I told him it's there if you want it." She handed me the dish. It was warm; it was a plain baked custard, with nutmeg. "I told him a number's like a cat. It doesn't come when you call it. But you can leave the door open."

I took the custard. I did not say anything. In the dusk behind her, the first stars were coming through the dust, one at a time, with the faint reddish cast they have in a dry spring, and the wind had dropped. In the creamery the light was still on. Joss had gone back to work. I could see his shadow on the card-covered wall, moving over the hundred and twelve little rectangles of Odd's rain, pausing at one and then another, as if he were reading them aloud.

"He's a nice boy," Hettie said.

"He's an auditor."

"He's both," she said. "It happens."
